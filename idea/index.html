Why Titraj?

The central questions in ordinary language

Titraj is the final novel’s name, but the word also became a way of keeping one electromagnetic phenomenon visible across different scales and vocabularies.

Event or message?

Does a distant event reach the observer—or only an electromagnetic consequence from which the event is reconstructed?

The event remains where it occurred. What arrives later is a physical disturbance carrying evidence of it. Keeping the source event, propagation, receiver and final judgment separate prevents the geometry of reception from being silently pushed backward into the completed event.

Light or photon?

Is a particle-like photon necessary for every explanation of what light does?

The companion begins with the electromagnetic wave and the response of matter. It asks whether some particle-like language describes a real travelling object or an effective account of a discrete material response. The successful calculations are not discarded; the physical picture assigned to them is examined.

One spectrum, many vocabularies

Why does the same electromagnetic spectrum acquire different explanatory nouns as its frequency changes?

Radio, microwave, visible light, X-rays and gamma rays are parts of one electromagnetic spectrum. Yet explanations shift from antenna currents to molecular vibration, electronic transition and photon impact. The question is whether the phenomenon changed—or whether different receivers and scales encouraged different stories.

Source or journey?

Does redshift tell us only about the source—or also about the history of the signal?

An observed spectrum is shaped by generation, propagation and reception. The portal asks whether every change in frequency, duration or structure should be assigned to the source, or whether part of the observation belongs to what happened while the signal travelled and to how it was finally sampled.

A correct calculation, a wrong object?

Can an equation remain successful while its physical interpretation is assigned to the wrong thing?

Book Two does not begin by denying the mathematics of special relativity. It asks whether a transformation developed to coordinate electromagnetic reports was interpreted as a transformation in the time of the distant events being reported. The calculation may work while the noun identifying its physical object changes.

What receives the light?

Is the receiver one isolated electron—or the organised material system to which that electron belongs?

A single electron is a useful first model, but it never moves independently of nearby nuclei, other electrons, geometry and local electromagnetic constraints. The later Titraj argument treats absorption, emission and possible escape as responses of organised many-electron matter rather than isolated targets.
