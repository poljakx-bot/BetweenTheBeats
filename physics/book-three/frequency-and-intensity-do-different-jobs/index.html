Frequency and Intensity Do Different Jobs

In the ordinary low-intensity photoelectric regime, changing frequency moves the threshold and maximum electron-energy edge, while changing intensity mainly changes how many emission events occur. That empirical separation is a sharp clue for any proposed receiver mechanism — but it is not universal, because nonlinear and strong-field regimes deliberately mix the two controls again.

Companion essay

Phenomenon → Prediction → Test

Frequency can select the response; intensity can scale participation — until the material enters a different regime.

The useful test is not simply to repeat the photoelectric law. It is to ask whether one mechanism can explain the ordinary separation, preserve the response fingerprint when only intensity changes, and predict the crossover into multiphoton or strong-field behaviour.

Intuition

Two knobs, two different ordinary effects

For a fixed material in the ordinary single-photon photoelectric regime, the maximum kinetic-energy edge follows

\[K_{\max}=h\nu-\phi.\]

Change \(\nu\), and the threshold and maximum electron energy change. Hold \(\nu\) fixed and increase intensity moderately, and the main effect is more photoemission events rather than a new value of the maximum single-event energy.

The same receiver is being driven in two experimentally different ways: frequency changes what output is allowed; intensity changes how much of that response occurs.

Titraj turns the distinction into a mechanism proposal

The working picture is:

\[\nu\rightarrow\text{response mode, geometry and allowed output},\]
\[I\rightarrow\text{drive strength, participation and event rate}.\]

This is not yet a derivation. It is a proposed physical division of labour that must reproduce the measured law.

At fixed frequency, the fingerprint should stay recognisable

If intensity merely increases participation in the same organised mode, then below the nonlinear crossover a fixed-frequency intensity sweep should mostly rescale the magnitude of the response while leaving its characteristic threshold, energy edge, polarisation preference and angular pattern approximately unchanged after ordinary heating, space-charge and detector effects are removed.

\[\boxed{\text{same mode}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{same normalised fingerprint, larger amplitude}.}\]

The rule has a boundary

At sufficiently high intensity, standard experiments show multiphoton emission, saturation, tunnelling and strong-field acceleration. Then intensity can change both the number and the energy distribution of emitted electrons.

A useful Titraj model must therefore predict not only the ordinary separation but also the point at which a new response regime becomes available.

The Argument

Begin with the measured low-intensity law

For a fixed surface,

\[eV_s=h\nu-\phi,\]

so the stopping potential and maximum kinetic-energy edge depend on frequency. In the ordinary unsaturated single-photon regime, the photoelectron yield at fixed \(\nu\) is approximately proportional to incident intensity:

\[R(\nu,I)\propto I\qquad(\nu\;\text{fixed, linear regime}).\]

These are empirical constraints, not optional features of an interpretation.

A useful factorisation

The ordinary regime can be represented schematically as

\[S(E,\Omega;\nu,I)\approx A(\nu,I)\,F(E,\Omega;\nu),\]

where \(A\) controls the total amount of response and \(F\) is the normalised energy-and-angle fingerprint at the chosen frequency.

If changing intensity only changes participation in one already selected mode, then

\[\frac{S(E,\Omega;\nu,I)}{\int S\,dE\,d\Omega}\approx F(E,\Omega;\nu)\]

should remain approximately invariant across a low-intensity sweep.

Frequency is the stronger selector

Changing frequency can cross a material threshold, alter the maximum kinetic-energy edge and, in the Titraj picture, select a different organised mode or path geometry. That means a frequency sweep can change not only amplitude but the structure of the response:

\[F(E,\Omega;\nu_1)\neq F(E,\Omega;\nu_2).\]

The mechanism-level proposal is therefore stronger than saying “higher frequency means more energy.” It says that frequency is a timing and mode-selection variable.

Intensity eventually changes the regime too

Standard nonlinear photoemission shows why the simple division cannot be universal. In an \(n\)-photon perturbative regime, yield can scale approximately as

\[R_n\propto I^n.\]

At still stronger fields, saturation and tunnelling can appear, and electron acceleration can introduce an intensity-dependent energy scale. A common strong-field scale is the ponderomotive energy, schematically

\[U_p\propto\frac{I}{\omega^2}.\]

So intensity can eventually alter the energy distribution as well as the count.

The prediction must therefore be conditional: frequency and intensity do different jobs inside a defined response regime; increasing intensity far enough can create a new regime.

The Titraj prediction

For a material and frequency range in which one organised mode is active, Titraj predicts a domain in intensity for which

\[\boxed{I\uparrow\;\Rightarrow\;\text{response amplitude rises while the normalised mode fingerprint remains stable}.}\]

When intensity reaches a mode-changing threshold, the fingerprint should change coherently — energy distribution, angular pattern, polarisation response or harmonic content should move together because the active material mode has changed.

Standard models already predict many nonlinear crossovers. Titraj becomes distinctive only if its proposed geometry predicts the crossover location or correlated fingerprint change more accurately or differently.

Deep Notes

The distinction between frequency and intensity is useful only if its domain is stated explicitly. In introductory discussions of the photoelectric effect, frequency sets the threshold and the maximum kinetic-energy edge while intensity controls the photoelectron current. That is a good summary of the ordinary low-intensity single-photon regime. It is not a universal law of light–matter interaction.

Modern nonlinear photoemission deliberately explores regimes in which several field interactions contribute to one emission event, the target saturates, tunnelling becomes relevant or the outgoing electron is accelerated by a strong oscillating field. In those regimes intensity can alter both yield and energy.

A mechanism proposal therefore has two obligations: explain the clean separation where it is observed, and explain why that separation breaks when the drive becomes strong enough.

The ordinary photoelectric constraint

Millikan's measurements established the linear stopping-potential relation with frequency:

\[eV_s=h\nu-\phi.\]

At a fixed frequency above threshold and sufficiently low intensity, increasing incident power mainly increases the number of emitted electrons. The maximum energy edge remains tied primarily to \(\nu\) and the material work function.

In the standard quantum description, this is naturally organised by the photon energy \(h\nu\) and the number of quanta incident per unit time. Path III keeps the measured separation but asks whether a continuous-field, organised-receiver mechanism can reproduce it without treating a simple impact cartoon as the only physical picture.

A mechanism-level factorisation

Let a particular frequency select an organised response mode \(M_\nu\). Write the observable schematically as

\[S(\xi;\nu,I)=N(\nu,I)\,G(\xi;M_\nu),\]

where \(\xi\) may include electron energy, emission angle, polarisation channel or another measured output coordinate. The proposed division is:

  • frequency selects or weights \(M_\nu\), thereby changing the allowed response geometry and output edge;
  • intensity changes how strongly or how often that selected mode is driven, represented first by \(N\).

In a genuinely linear domain, the normalised shape

\[\widehat S(\xi;\nu,I)=\frac{S(\xi;\nu,I)}{\int S(\xi;\nu,I)d\xi}\]

should be approximately independent of intensity at fixed \(\nu\).

What would make this more than standard linear response

Linear-response theory already predicts that sufficiently weak driving scales the response without changing the underlying susceptibility. Therefore invariance of a normalised fingerprint at low intensity is a benchmark, not unique evidence for Titraj.

A distinct mechanism-level prediction requires more. If Titraj supplies a real-space mode geometry, it should predict a specific set of correlated observables that remain locked together while intensity changes and then reorganise together at a calculable crossover:

\[\{K_{edge},\,P(\theta),\,\Pi,\,\mathbf H\}_{M_\nu}.\]

Here \(P(\theta)\) is an angular distribution, \(\Pi\) a polarisation signature and \(\mathbf H\) a harmonic fingerprint.

Multiphoton behaviour is not a contradiction; it is a boundary condition

In perturbative multiphoton ionisation or photoemission, an \(n\)-interaction channel commonly gives a yield scaling close to

\[R_n\propto I^n.\]

Experiments also show saturation when the available target population is depleted. A complete Titraj account would have to derive these nonlinear powers or provide an alternative quantitative law that fits the same data.

Strong fields also move the energy scale

In strong optical fields, the electron can acquire an additional cycle-averaged energy scale. For a monochromatic field, the standard ponderomotive scale has the dependence

\[U_p\propto\frac{I}{\omega^2}.\]

Experiments in plasmonically enhanced photoemission show intensity-dependent electron acceleration in the strong-field regime. Thus the statement “intensity never changes electron energy” would be experimentally false.

The useful claim is narrower:

\[\boxed{\text{within one weak-response mode, frequency selects the fingerprint while intensity primarily scales participation}.}\]

The experiment

Choose a material and frequency comfortably inside an ordinary linear regime. Sweep intensity over a range that avoids heating, space charge, saturation and known multiphoton onset. Record not only total yield but the full normalised output fingerprint.

Then repeat the sweep across the onset of nonlinear behaviour. A mode-based theory should predict which observables change first and whether the transition occurs as one coherent reorganisation or as independent conventional nonlinear effects.

The standard description supplies the baseline. Titraj must predict a reproducible residual or a geometry-linked crossover before the data are fitted.

The boundary

\[\boxed{\text{frequency/intensity separation is a regime-dependent phenomenon, not a universal rule}.}\]

That makes it more useful, not less: a physical mechanism has to explain both the clean separation and the experimentally observed conditions under which it fails.
