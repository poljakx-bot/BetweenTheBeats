Path III · Theme 7

From Antenna to Matter: What Sets the Size of a Receiver?

The half-wave dipole is a resonant benchmark. It is not a universal ruler that every material receiver must physically match.

Published essay

Three lengths to keep separate

Physical size, electrical size and path length are different quantities.

A receiver can be physically small, electrically resonant through its material and geometry, and still support an internal dynamical path whose total arc length is larger than its end-to-end dimension.

Intuition

The antenna analogy is useful until it becomes a ruler

A familiar half-wave dipole has a resonant scale near

\[L\sim\frac{\lambda}{2}.\]

That can tempt us to say that any efficient electromagnetic receiver must literally be half a wavelength long.

That conclusion is too strong. Real resonators depend on geometry, material response, boundaries, effective wavelength, loading and the mode being excited. A subwavelength object can still couple to an electromagnetic field.

\(\lambda/2\) is an important dynamical scale. It is not automatically the physical length of the receiver.

The Path III question

The portal uses the antenna analogy for one reason: organised charge motion and geometry matter. It does not claim that a molecule is a tiny metal rod.

The stronger Titraj hypothesis asks a different question. If a participating charge follows a constrained path at local speed \(c\), then over half a field cycle the total arc length would be

\[s_{1/2}=\frac{c}{2f}=\frac{\lambda}{2}.\]

That path can be curved, folded or recurrent. Its end-to-end displacement can be much smaller than its total length.

The Argument

1. Start with the ordinary half-wave benchmark

For a free-space wavelength

\[\lambda=\frac{c}{f},\]

a simple half-wave dipole has an efficient resonant geometry of order

\[L\sim\frac{\lambda}{2}.\]

The exact resonant length depends on conductor geometry, environment and end effects. The relation is therefore already a physical approximation, not a universal identity.

2. Electrical length need not equal physical length

A resonant structure responds according to the phase accumulated by its mode, not simply the ruler length of the object. In a material or guided structure one may write schematically

\[\lambda_{\rm eff}=\frac{2\pi}{k_{\rm eff}},\]

where \(k_{\rm eff}\) includes the medium and geometry. A physically short structure can therefore be electrically long enough to support a resonance.

3. Matter is not a miniature wire antenna

A molecule or solid has bound charges, quantised material states, screening, nuclei, surfaces and many-body constraints. The antenna analogy should retain only the useful physical lesson:

\[\text{EM drive}+\text{organised charge}+\text{geometry}\rightarrow\text{mode-selective response}.\]

It should not import wire-current geometry literally into matter.

4. The half-cycle propagation scale

One period of a field at frequency \(f\) lasts

\[T=\frac{1}{f}.\]

Half a cycle lasts \(1/(2f)\). A disturbance travelling at speed \(c\) covers

\[cT_{1/2}=\frac{c}{2f}=\frac{\lambda}{2}.\]

This identity is kinematic. What Path III adds is the conjecture that an internal charge path may use this interval as a dynamical arc-length constraint.

5. Path length is not displacement

For a curved path \(\mathbf r(s)\), the arc length can satisfy

\[s_{1/2}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\]

while the endpoint separation obeys

\[|\Delta\mathbf r|\ll s_{1/2}.\]

This is how a physically small receiver could, in principle, contain a longer dynamical path. The proposal requires an actual geometry and equations of motion; the inequality alone does not prove one exists.

6. Two hypotheses must remain separate

The organised many-electron receiver hypothesis needs only that coupled matter carries relevant pre-output state.

The local-\(c\) path hypothesis additionally requires

\[\left|d\mathbf s/dt\right|=c.\]

The second is much stronger. Subwavelength optical response can support the first while falsifying the second.

7. What a real model must predict

A successful geometry must predict resonance frequency, polarisation dependence, angular response, linewidth and how those quantities change when the physical receiver size or surrounding dielectric environment is altered.

If \(s_{1/2}=c/(2f)\) is only restated after the measured frequency is known, it has explained nothing.

Deep Notes

This section starts from antenna resonance and then carefully separates three lengths. No prior knowledge of Titraj is assumed.

1. Physical length

The physical length \(L_{\rm phys}\) is a ruler measurement of the object. For a simple thin-wire half-wave dipole in free space, resonance occurs at a length of order \(\lambda/2\), with practical corrections.

2. Electrical length

The electromagnetic mode depends on accumulated phase. A useful description uses a propagation constant \(k_{\rm eff}\), giving

\[\lambda_{\rm eff}=\frac{2\pi}{k_{\rm eff}}.\]

Material permittivity, confinement, geometry and loading can make \(\lambda_{\rm eff}\) differ substantially from the free-space wavelength. Therefore

\[L_{\rm phys}\neq L_{\rm electrical}\]

in general.

3. Dynamical path length

A third quantity is the distance travelled along an internal trajectory during some interval:

\[s=\int |d\mathbf r|.\]

This can exceed the straight-line displacement between the endpoints. A folded or curved path can therefore have a large arc length inside a compact region.

4. Where \(c/(2f)\) enters

The half-period of a drive is \(1/(2f)\). If an internal path is assumed to have local speed \(c\), then

\[s_{1/2}=\int_0^{1/(2f)}c\,dt=\frac{c}{2f}.\]

Since \(\lambda=c/f\), this becomes \(\lambda/2\).

The equality follows only after the local-speed assumption is made. It is therefore a consequence of H3, not independent evidence for H3.

5. Why subwavelength emitters are a constraint rather than a contradiction

A small physical device can radiate or absorb at wavelengths much larger than its own dimensions. That means any antenna-inspired matter model must distinguish physical size from mode scale. It does not automatically decide whether a hidden internal path exists.

The correct test is whether the proposed path predicts observables not already inserted through the measured frequency.

6. What survives without local-c

Even if no internal charge follows a path at \(c\), the antenna analogy can still support the weaker statement that organised charge response has geometry, phase and resonance. That weaker many-electron receiver is independent of the local-c conjecture.

7. Open obligation

The local-c path hypothesis must eventually provide an explicit trajectory family and derive, from material structure, which paths are allowed. It must then predict frequency, polarisation, angular emission or reception pattern and their dependence on geometry.

Until then,

\[\boxed{\frac{c}{2f}=\frac{\lambda}{2}}\]

is a useful scale and a proposed clue—not yet a microscopic theory.
