Path III · Theme 5

Absorption, Emission and Escape

Source and receiver are organised material systems. Emission and absorption are opposite handoffs; escape is one possible local ending.

Published essay

Three stages to keep separate

Source mode, propagation and receiver output are not one object.

Standard quantum theory successfully relates material transitions and electromagnetic modes. Path III keeps the numerical constraints while asking what physical organisation exists at each boundary and what, if anything, must be localised during propagation.

Intuition

Two material boundaries and one electromagnetic link

At the source, organised matter changes and an outgoing electromagnetic disturbance is produced. At the receiver, an incoming electromagnetic disturbance changes organised matter.

\[\text{source mode}\rightarrow\text{EM propagation}\rightarrow\text{receiver mode}.\]

The receiver can then retain the new state, redistribute energy, drive a current, heat a lattice, emit again or open an escape channel through which one electron leaves.

Path III treats emission and absorption as opposite directions of the same field–matter problem. The local discreteness appears at the material handoff. The propagation history does not automatically have to be pictured as a box containing a tiny travelling object.

One electron may complete the escape. The mode that prepared the escape can involve organised matter on a larger scale.

Visible light is not reduced to an isolated atomic dipole

The portal’s current working picture treats ordinary optical emission and reception as molecular or molecular-scale many-electron modes. The familiar antenna analogy is therefore about organised charge response and geometry, not about one electron behaving as a miniature straight metal rod.

Frequency determines the timescale and helps select the allowed mode. Intensity changes how strongly or how often that mode participates. The detailed geometry is a stronger hypothesis considered explicitly in the final theme.

The Argument

1. Standard quantum bookkeeping remains the benchmark

For a transition between material states, standard quantum theory relates the transition frequency and energy difference through

\[|E_f-E_i|=h\nu.\]

In emission the material loses energy while the electromagnetic field gains it; in absorption the direction is reversed. The portal does not discard the quantitative constraint. It asks what physical mechanism the state labels compress.

2. Emission begins in prepared matter

A source state can contain electronic occupation, charge redistribution, molecular or lattice geometry, polarisation, local fields and correlations among participating regions. A transition changes that organisation and produces an outgoing electromagnetic field.

In the portal’s working picture, visible-light emission is first modelled as an organised many-electron mode. It is not assumed to be one isolated electron making a classical orbit change.

3. A laser makes source organisation unavoidable

Laser output depends on a prepared population of material systems capable of contributing to a common electromagnetic mode, together with cavity geometry, losses, pumping and phase organisation.

Standard theory writes the energy of a mode in quantised form and interprets its occupation through photon number. Path III keeps that formal description as a quantitative benchmark while asking whether the physical source process can be described more directly as repeated organised material contributions to a common field mode.

The portal therefore uses the cautious schematic relation

\[\text{more permitted participating transitions}\rightarrow\text{greater available field output},\]

not the stronger claim that one scalar electron count alone determines intensity.

4. Stimulated emission is a coupled source–field response

An existing field influences the probability and mode of a source transition. The added contribution is constrained by the resonant mode, polarisation, phase relation and geometry. This already makes emission a coupled process rather than a factory ejecting independent classical pellets.

5. Spontaneous emission is an open physical obligation

A prepared excited state can emit without an external trigger at the moment of emission. In the portal’s proposed picture, the source mode itself provides the prepared physical structure whose internal evolution can terminate in an outgoing electromagnetic field.

This is not enough as an explanation. A successful model must derive lifetime statistics, linewidth, selection rules, polarisation, angular distribution and environmental modification. If those quantities cannot be recovered, the proposed mechanism fails.

6. Absorption is the reverse handoff

At the receiver, the field drives an organised material state. The response can remain reversible or can end in a completed transition that stores or redistributes energy.

\[\text{incoming EM field}\rightarrow\text{receiver-state evolution}\rightarrow\text{completed transfer or relaxation}.\]

The final record may be one electron, but the relevant receiving state before escape can be distributed.

7. Escape is not identical to absorption

Electron escape is one possible local completion. The receiver may first develop a many-electron redistribution, transfer energy through the material and only later open a specific surface channel.

The emitted electron must satisfy the observed energy and momentum constraints. The portal does not gain freedom to ignore conservation merely because it replaces a particle-impact story.

8. Frequency and intensity are assigned different first roles

The working distinction is

\[\nu\rightarrow\text{mode, timescale and allowed geometry},\]
\[I\rightarrow\text{response magnitude, participation or event probability}.\]

Strong-drive nonlinearities can couple these roles. The distinction is intended for the ordinary regime in which frequency and intensity effects are empirically separable.

Deep Notes

This section starts with two material systems connected by an electromagnetic field. It builds the emission–propagation–absorption chain without assuming either a classical pellet or the portal’s stronger trajectory conjecture.

1. Source, field and receiver are separate physical layers

A complete model must specify the prepared source state, the propagating electromagnetic state and the prepared receiver state. A successful transition formula does not erase those distinctions.

\[S(t)\rightarrow F(\mathbf r,t)\rightarrow R(t)\rightarrow O.\]

Here \(O\) is the local output: an electron, a current pulse, an emitted field or another record.

2. Emission need not mean a miniature projectile is born

Standard field quantisation describes emission as creation of an excitation of the electromagnetic field. Path III does not challenge the numerical success of that description. It challenges only the unnecessary additional image that the excitation must behave as a pre-existing point object following one classical path from source to receiver.

3. Organised optical modes

The portal’s present physical hypothesis is that visible-light source and receiver modes are molecular or molecular-scale many-electron organisations. Their charge motion is constrained by surrounding electrons, nuclei and geometry. This makes the antenna analogy a statement about organised mode structure rather than a claim that one atom is a scaled-down wire antenna.

4. The half-wave scale is not yet a size law

For a frequency \(f\), the vacuum half-wavelength is

\[\frac{\lambda}{2}=\frac{c}{2f}.\]

Path III later uses this as a candidate dynamical path scale in its stronger geometry hypothesis. It should not be confused with a universal minimum physical size of an emitter or receiver. Subwavelength structures can couple and radiate; geometry, effective wavelength, material dispersion and mode confinement matter.

5. Intensity requires both material population and field organisation

More available participating electronic systems can increase the possible output, but measured intensity also depends on field amplitude, coherence, mode volume, cavity loss, coupling and pump dynamics. The portal therefore treats “number of participating electrons” as one physical control, not as a complete laser equation.

6. Spontaneous emission is a decisive test

A source-only physical account must recover the observed statistics and mode properties without inserting an unexplained external trigger. If the organised material mode cannot reproduce those quantities, standard field quantisation remains necessary at precisely that point.

7. Reception and escape can have different boundaries

The region that first receives the field need not be identical to the site where the final electron leaves. Energy, phase and charge redistribution can propagate through the material before a local surface channel completes.

This is a testable statement only if the proposed receiver state predicts controlled differences that a matched local output model misses.

8. What this theme establishes

\[\boxed{\text{organised source}\leftrightarrow\text{EM field}\leftrightarrow\text{organised receiver}}\]

is the minimum physical chain explored by Titraj. The final theme states the stronger geometric hypotheses separately and defines what would survive if they fail.
