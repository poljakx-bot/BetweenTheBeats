Path III · Theme 5

Absorption, Emission and Escape

Source and receiver are material systems. Emission, absorption and escape are organised changes connected by an electromagnetic field.

Published essay

Four questions to keep separate

Measurement is not the same thing as its explanation.

As you read, keep four tasks distinct: the standard account; what the measurement directly establishes; the portal’s objection or alternative; and the evidence that could distinguish the two.

Intuition

Absorption, emission and electron escape are often taught as separate mechanisms.

In the Titraj continuity, they are different directions and endings of the same coupled process.

At a source, organised matter changes state and an outgoing electromagnetic field follows.

At a receiver, an incoming electromagnetic field changes organised matter.

The receiver may then:

  • retain the new state;
  • redistribute energy;
  • transfer it into a lattice or current;
  • emit a new field;
  • open a channel through which one electron escapes.

The discrete step belongs to the material transition or completed field–matter transfer.

The field between source and receiver need not be pictured as a box carrying a little object from one boundary to the other.

This makes emission and absorption physically symmetric:

\[\text{material mode change} \\ \leftrightarrow \\ \text{electromagnetic mode change}.\]

The Argument

Let a material system change between states with energy difference

\[\Delta E=E_i-E_f=h\nu.\]

In emission, the material loses this energy and an electromagnetic output at frequency \(\nu\) is generated.

In absorption, the field drives the material into a higher-energy or otherwise changed permitted configuration.

The established quantum calculation remains intact. The interpretive question is what the integer step counts.

Does it count independently travelling objects?

Or does it count permitted elementary transfers between an organised material mode and an electromagnetic mode?

Emission: a transition of the source

A prepared source contains stored material energy and organisation.

Its state may involve:

  • electronic occupation;
  • charge redistribution;
  • polarisation;
  • molecular or lattice geometry;
  • local and extended electromagnetic fields;
  • correlations among participating regions.

A transition changes that organisation and produces an outgoing electromagnetic field.

The source-side discreteness is directly tied to a material change.

The standard account calls the corresponding field increment the creation of one photon.

Titraj asks whether this statement must be read ontologically as the birth of a new localised object, or whether it can be read as exact energy bookkeeping for one completed source-to-field transfer.

The laser makes the material bookkeeping explicit

A laser does not calculate its available output from an abstract photon reservoir alone.

Its gain depends on the population of material emitters capable of the relevant transition. A population inversion supplies the number of available downward transitions. The field mode, cavity losses, coupling and pumping determine how those transitions develop into output power.

Schematically, the stored material energy available to the laser transition is

\[U_{\mathrm{stored}} \\ \sim \\ N_{\mathrm{available}}h\nu.\]

A stimulated transition changes the material population and increases the energy of the common resonant electromagnetic mode by

\[\Delta U_{\mathrm{mode}}=h\nu.\]

The standard formalism writes

\[U_{\mathrm{mode}}=nh\nu\]

and interprets \(n\) as the photon occupation number of the mode.

Titraj does not alter the rate equations or the measured output. It asks what the integer means physically.

It may count:

  • field quanta as independent excitations;

or, in the proposed interpretation,

  • the completed material transitions that have contributed fixed energy increments to the same organised field mode.

The crucial laser fact is that the number of electronic systems able to participate is already an explicit part of the intensity and gain calculation.

An additional permitted transition does not create an arbitrary field. It contributes to a mode with an established frequency, phase relation, polarisation, direction and cavity geometry.

Thus the alternative reading is:

\[\boxed{ \\ \text{one additional permitted material transition} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{one }h\nu\text{ increment of the common EM mode} \\ }\]

rather than necessarily

\[\boxed{ \\ \text{one new localised object begins an independent journey} \\ }.\]

Stimulated emission is organised source response

In stimulated emission, an existing field mode influences the timing and mode of a source transition.

The new contribution matches the relevant mode properties. This is naturally described as a change in a coupled source–field organisation.

The emitter is not merely a factory ejecting identical pellets. Its allowed transition and the existing mode are dynamically related.

Spontaneous emission

A source can emit without an external trigger at the moment of emission.

In the Titraj picture, “spontaneous” means:

not externally initiated at that instant,

not

physically unprepared or causeless.

A prepared excited material state can be an organised Titraj. Its internal evolution can end in a transition to another mode with an outgoing electromagnetic field.

But this cannot be reduced to a little classical antenna ringing down. A successful account must reproduce:

  • lifetime distributions;
  • linewidth;
  • selection rules;
  • polarisation;
  • angular pattern;
  • stimulated emission;
  • environmental modification, including cavity effects.

If those constraints do not follow, the physical picture is incomplete even if it is intuitive.

Absorption: the reverse handoff

At the receiver, the incoming field couples to the organised material state.

A completed absorption step changes occupation, correlation, polarisation or another permitted receiver mode.

The system may then redistribute the received energy before a final record appears.

Thus “absorption” should not be compressed into

a photon vanished inside one electron.

A more complete handoff is

\[\text{incoming field state} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{receiver-state change} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{later output or relaxation}.\]

The receiver may store information about amplitude, phase, timing and geometry before the final event becomes local.

Escape: one local ending of a distributed history

Electron escape is not synonymous with absorption.

The receiver may first develop a distributed electronic and structural response. Only later does one local escape channel become available.

The output electron can carry kinetic energy and directional information. Its final state must satisfy the established conservation laws.

But the experiment need not have selected that particular labelled electron when the field first arrived.

The canonical statement is:

One electron completes the escape. The receiving history that made the escape probable may have belonged to the organised material state.

Deep Notes

1. Intensity is not only the number of available electrons

The number of available transitions sets the gain and stored-energy scale, but instantaneous laser intensity also depends on transition rate, field amplitude, mode volume, cavity loss, output coupling, coherence and pump dynamics.

The Titraj point is not that one scalar electron count determines every laser observable. It is that the material population is indispensable and that each field-energy increment is tied to a permitted material transition.

2. Coherent contributions do not add only as independent powers

Fields add as amplitudes:

\[\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{total}}=\sum_j\mathbf{E}_j,\]

while intensity depends on

\[I\propto |\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{total}}|^2.\]

Phase organisation can enhance, suppress or redirect the result. Therefore a new participant does not always add the same raw intensity outside the defined mode and measurement geometry.

The fixed \(h\nu\) increment refers to the energy accounting of an elementary transition in the mode, not to a universal step in every measured intensity trace.

3. Source and receiver are roles, not permanent identities

The same material system may receive during preparation and emit later. Calling it first a receiver and then a source describes chronology, not necessarily two physical objects.

The more fundamental object is the prepared material state and its allowed transformations.

4. Energy conservation is retained event by event

Reinterpreting the photon does not permit average-only accounting where experiments require event-wise conservation. Emitted fields, recoil, outgoing electrons and residual material states must satisfy the same quantitative energy–momentum constraints.

5. The field cannot be empty

Source correlations survive propagation and affect receiver statistics. Titraj therefore does not claim that all quantum structure is manufactured by matter at the final detector.

It seeks a joint source–field–receiver account in which the field carries real structure without requiring a classical pellet trajectory.

6. What this theme establishes

Emission and absorption can be represented as opposite handoffs between organised material modes and electromagnetic modes.

Escape is one possible local completion after reception.

The remaining task is to define exactly what deserves the name Titraj.
