Path III · Theme 3

No Electron Responds Alone

A final electron may be local. The receiving state that made that output possible can be distributed across organised matter.

Published essay

Core hypothesis

The output carrier and the receiver are not automatically the same object.

Path III treats the smallest useful receiver as an experimentally defined coupled state. The final electron can remain a valid local output while the preparation that determines its probability belongs to a larger many-electron mode.

Intuition

The background was doing the work

Begin with one electron. Ask what binds it, what local field acts on it, what directions are allowed, which states are occupied, what barrier must be crossed and where absorbed energy can go.

Nearly every answer points outside the electron.

The selected electron may be the place where the final record becomes local, but its earlier response depends on neighbouring charge, nuclei, geometry, surfaces and the evolving electromagnetic field of the material itself.

The final carrier can be individual while the permission to become that carrier is structural.

What “many-electron” means here

It does not mean every electron moves in phase. It does not mean a classical cloud follows one orbit. It does not mean the receiver must extend across the whole object.

It means that reducing the interaction to one active electron plus a passive background can discard predictive information that was physically present before the event.

For visible-light reception, the portal’s present working picture is a molecular or molecular-scale organised mode: several electrons are constrained by the same material structure, and the field couples to that organised response rather than to a lone bead.

The Argument

1. The local field is state-dependent

For a selected electronic region \(i\), write schematically

\[\mathbf E_{{\rm local},i}=\mathbf E_{\rm incident}+\mathbf E_{\rm nuclei,i}+\mathbf E_{\rm electrons,i}+\mathbf E_{\rm boundaries,i}+\mathbf E_{\rm induced,i}.\]

As the material redistributes charge, the local field changes. That altered local field changes the next possible response.

\[\text{receiver state}\rightarrow\text{local response}\rightarrow\text{new receiver state}.\]

The interaction is therefore self-consistent and coupled.

2. Replace one coordinate with a receiver state

A generic pre-event receiver state may be written

\[R(t)=\{A_i(t),\phi_i(t),n_i(t),V_i(t),C_{ij}(t),L(t),G\},\]

where the variables stand, according to the apparatus, for response amplitudes, phase relations, occupations, local potentials, couplings, lattice or nuclear state, and geometry.

The notation is not a claim that every experiment must reconstruct every microscopic variable. It is a reminder that an output model should inherit whatever preparation variables are required for the prediction it makes.

3. The receiver boundary is empirical

The receiver should not be enlarged indefinitely whenever a local model fails. A useful operational definition is:

The receiver is the smallest coupled state that must be retained before the output in order to preserve a tested prediction across controlled changes of geometry and preparation.

If a one-electron effective model predicts equally well, the wider state is unnecessary for that claim. If matched local conditions produce different output statistics when distant-but-coupled preparation is changed, the receiver must expand.

4. Frequency and intensity have different first roles

In the portal’s working picture, frequency helps select which organised mode and geometry can respond, while intensity changes response magnitude, number of participating local regions or event probability:

\[\nu\rightarrow\text{available mode},\qquad I\rightarrow\text{participation or response magnitude}.\]

This is a hypothesis to be tested, not a universal law. Strong fields can reshape the receiver and open nonlinear channels.

5. The final event may still be one electron

The preparation can be distributed while the completed output is local:

\[\text{distributed receiver state}\rightarrow\text{local output channel}\rightarrow\text{discrete record}.\]

The mistake is not using a one-electron output equation. The mistake is using the local output to infer that the whole previous receiving history belonged to that same individually identifiable electron.

6. Keep the trajectory hypothesis separate

The many-electron receiver hypothesis does not require any specific microscopic path. In particular, it does not require the later conjecture that a participating electron locally moves at \(c\) along a constrained three-dimensional path.

\[H_1:\ \text{organised many-electron receiver},\]
\[H_2:\ \text{specific local-}c\text{ trajectory}.\]

\(H_2\) is stronger. Rejecting it would not automatically reject \(H_1\).

Deep Notes

This section defines the many-electron receiver without relying on the previous themes. It starts from local electromagnetic coupling, introduces the minimum coupled state, and states exactly what would make the hypothesis useful or unnecessary.

1. A local force does not imply a local receiver

Electromagnetic interactions act locally on charge, but the local field and allowed motion can depend on the state of surrounding matter. A local final event is therefore compatible with a distributed preparation history.

2. “Collective” is deliberately restricted

Here collective means only that a one-electron reduction loses relevant joint information. It does not require global phase coherence, identical trajectories, a rigid charge cloud or an unlimited receiver boundary.

The relevant region may be a molecule, a molecular-scale cluster, a band-state region, a surface mode or another experimentally determined coupled structure.

3. Effective one-electron models remain legitimate

Many-body influence is often compressed into an effective potential, band structure, self-energy, damping term or transition matrix element. Path III does not reject this. It asks what information was compressed and whether that compression remains adequate when the preparation geometry changes.

4. The minimal-receiver experiment

A discriminating test would prepare two systems with matched local output variables but different coupled receiver histories. If the one-electron model predicts identical outcomes while the organised-state model predicts different distributions, blind comparison can determine whether the extra receiver state carries transferable information.

The hypothesis earns relevance only if the added state improves prospective prediction before the result is known.

5. Surfaces and escape

For photoemission, the output depends on more than an electron’s local energy. Surface composition, orientation, work function, transport, local fields and available escape channels matter. The emitted electron is individual; the conditions that make escape possible are structural.

6. Many-electron reception is the core; local-c is optional

The core Path III claim is that organised matter can be the receiver. A later geometric model may propose a particular constrained electron path and a half-cycle path scale related to \(c/(2f)\). That is an additional conjecture, not part of the minimum receiver definition.

This separation matters scientifically. If detailed trajectory measurements reject the stronger geometry, the receiver hypothesis should stand or fall on its own predictive tests.

7. What this theme establishes

\[\boxed{\text{one electron at the output}\;\not\Rightarrow\;\text{one-electron receiving history}.}\]

The next theme asks what the photoelectric measurements actually constrain once that distinction is kept visible.
