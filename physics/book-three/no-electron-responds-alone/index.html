Path III · Theme 3

No Electron Responds Alone

The decisive correction: the field may act locally, but the allowed response belongs to organised matter.

Published essay

Four questions to keep separate

Measurement is not the same thing as its explanation.

As you read, keep four tasks distinct: the standard account; what the measurement directly establishes; the portal’s objection or alternative; and the evidence that could distinguish the two.

Intuition

Jan’s first equation described one electron and treated the rest of the material as a background.

Then the background began doing all the work.

It supplied the binding.

It supplied the local field.

It supplied the available states.

It supplied the route by which energy could leave the initial response.

It supplied the surface through which an electron might escape.

The selected electron was explicit. The receiver was hidden.

The correction is not simply that “many electrons matter.” Standard many-body physics already knows that coupled systems are coupled.

The sharper claim is methodological:

A one-electron output model must declare which receiver state it inherited. It cannot erase the organised material history and then treat the final carrier as though it had always been the complete receiver.

No electron inside matter responds independently of the changing charge distribution, occupation, geometry and electromagnetic fields produced by the others.

That does not mean every electron moves in phase.

It does not mean a classical crowd orbit.

It means that the allowed response of each relevant region depends materially on the state of other regions over the timescale of the interaction.

The Argument

For electron \(i\), the local field can be decomposed schematically as

\[\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{local},i} \\ = \\ \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{incident}} \\ + \\ \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{nuclei},i} \\ + \\ \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{electrons},i} \\ + \\ \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{boundaries},i} \\ + \\ \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{induced},i}.\]

As the electronic distribution changes, the local fields change. Those fields alter the next possible redistribution.

Thus:

\[\text{state determines local response},\]

while

\[\text{local responses change the state}.\]

The receiver is self-consistent and coupled.

From one variable to a receiver state

Instead of one displacement \(x(t)\), the relevant pre-event state may require a set of variables

\[R(t)=\{A_i(t),\phi_i(t),n_i(t),V_i(t),C_{ij}(t),L(t),G\},\]

where the symbols may represent, depending on the apparatus:

  • response amplitude in region \(i\);
  • phase relative to the drive;
  • occupation or charge distribution;
  • local potential or barrier;
  • coupling between regions;
  • lattice or nuclear state where relevant;
  • geometry and boundaries.

The model need not literally measure every microscopic variable. It must retain the variables required for the claim it makes.

For a later escape channel \(i\), one may write schematically

\[P_i \\ = \\ F\bigl(R_i(t),W_i,S_i,E_{\mathrm{drive}}\bigr).\]

The important point is not the undefined function \(F\). It is the comparison between models.

A local model assumes that the final site plus an effective background contains all relevant preparation information.

A distributed receiver model predicts that phase, coupling or state elsewhere changes the probability distribution over later local outputs, even when local output variables are matched.

That difference is experimentally testable.

The strongest constraint may be other electrons

A static nucleus-centred potential is often not enough to describe what an electron can do in a material.

The changing positions, occupations and electromagnetic influence of the other electrons may provide the strongest immediate constraint on the relevant timescale.

But the electronic network is not automatically the whole receiver.

The response may pass into nuclei, lattice motion, defects, surfaces or external circuitry and return before the final event. The receiver boundary must expand only when measured coupling requires it—and the expansion must make a new prediction.

Otherwise “the receiver is larger” becomes an unfalsifiable repair.

The final event may still be local

The preparation can be distributed while the completed output is local.

A detector may register:

  • one transferred elementary charge;
  • one electron leaving a surface;
  • one current avalanche;
  • one emitted field event.

The final local model can remain valid if it inherits sufficient state from the organised receiver.

This gives the canonical handoff:

\[\text{distributed receiver state} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{local output channel} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{discrete record}.\]

The mistake is not using a one-electron output model.

The mistake is pretending that the output model proves the receiver was always one electron.

Receiver dependence is predictive, not omnipotent

The manuscript’s strongest supported claim is probabilistic:

The probability and location of a later single-electron escape may be constrained by an evolving receiver state extending across several electronic regions and, where the data require it, coupled structural degrees of freedom before any particular output electron can be identified.

This does not say the receiver state uniquely determines the individual event.

It says that removing the joint structure can reduce prospective prediction.

Deep Notes

1. “Collective” has a restricted meaning

It does not require:

  • all electrons moving together;
  • one classical trajectory for a charge cloud;
  • coherence across the whole object;
  • an indefinitely large receiver.

It means that a reduction to one active electron plus passive background loses relevant, transferable predictive information.

2. Effective one-electron models can remain excellent

An effective potential can compress many-body influence into a tractable equation. The portal does not reject that method.

It asks the model to declare what was compressed and under which conditions the compression fails.

3. Surfaces are part of the receiver

The work function, escape channels and final electron-energy distribution depend on surface composition, orientation, contamination, local fields and transport to the boundary.

The emitted electron is individual. The permission to escape is structural.

4. The receiver is defined experimentally

A useful operational definition is:

The receiver is the smallest coupled state that must be retained before the output in order to preserve the tested prediction across controlled changes of geometry and measurement.

This boundary is claim-dependent, not metaphysically fixed in advance.

5. The minimal-receiver test

A discriminating experiment should compare two models that agree on local output variables but differ in joint preparation or coupling geometry.

The organised model earns relevance only if its additional state improves blind, transferable prediction.

6. What this theme establishes

Organised matter is the receiver. The final electron is one possible output carrier.

That distinction prepares the central question of the book: what does the photoelectric experiment actually establish about the radiation before the receiver completed its response?
