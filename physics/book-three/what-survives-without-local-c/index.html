Path III · Theme 11

What Survives If the Local-c Path Is Wrong?

A research programme should know which claims stand independently. The local-c trajectory is the strongest Titraj conjecture, not the foundation on which every weaker claim must depend.

Published essay

Hypothesis hierarchy

Do not make the strongest claim carry the weaker ones.

Path III separates a core organised-receiver claim, a frequency-selected mode claim and a much stronger local-c trajectory claim. Each must be allowed to succeed or fail on its own evidence.

Intuition

Titraj contains claims of different strength

The broadest idea is that organised matter can be the physically relevant electromagnetic receiver before a final local electron or detector event appears.

A stronger idea is that frequency selects a particular dynamical geometry within that receiver.

The strongest idea is that an electron participating in that mode follows a constrained path with local speed \(c\).

\[H_1\subsetneq H_2\subsetneq H_3\quad\text{in commitment, not necessarily in truth}.\]

The mistake would be to treat these as one indivisible statement.

A failed path should be removed, not rescued by renaming

If an experiment or calculation shows that the local-c trajectory cannot reproduce momentum, timing, radiation or material constraints, H3 should be discarded.

That would also remove any result that depended specifically on it, such as interpreting

\[\frac{c}{2f}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\]

as the arc length travelled by one electron during a half-cycle.

But it would not automatically answer whether organised many-electron state, geometry or frequency-selective response is physically relevant.

A useful theory should become smaller when a strong conjecture fails, not become harder to falsify.

The Argument

1. H1: the organised-receiver claim

H1 states that the physically relevant pre-output state may extend beyond the final electron or detector carrier.

\[\text{organised receiver state}\rightarrow\text{local output}.\]

This claim concerns what state variables must be retained to predict the event. It does not require a particular microscopic trajectory.

H1 survives only if the distributed receiver state makes transferable predictions that a local reduction does not.

2. H2: the frequency-selected mode claim

H2 adds that different frequencies preferentially couple to different dynamical modes or geometries of the receiver.

\[\nu\rightarrow M_\nu(R_0,G).\]

The notation means that frequency, prepared receiver state and geometry select or weight a family of allowed responses.

H2 does not require one electron to traverse a path at \(c\). A collective current, polarisation mode, charge redistribution or other structured response could supply the geometry.

3. H3: the constrained local-c trajectory

H3 adds a specific microscopic statement:

\[\left|\dot{\mathbf r}(t)\right|=c\]

along a constrained internal path.

If maintained over a half-cycle, it gives the arc-length relation

\[s_{1/2}=\int_0^{1/(2f)}|\dot{\mathbf r}(t)|dt=\frac{c}{2f}=\frac{\lambda}{2}.\]

This equation is therefore conditional on H3. It is not a general consequence of H1 or H2.

4. What would falsify only H3?

Suppose a many-electron model predicts the correct frequency, polarisation and angular response, but direct dynamical constraints require subluminal participating charge motion. Then H3 fails while H1 and possibly H2 can remain.

Likewise, if the measured momentum distribution cannot be generated by the proposed local-c path family but can be generated by another organised receiver mode, the path should be removed rather than used as a metaphor.

5. What would falsify H2 while leaving H1?

Suppose distributed receiver state matters, but its relevant modes do not scale with frequency in the proposed geometric way. Then the many-electron receiver survives while the special frequency-to-geometry mapping fails.

H1 would then become a more modest claim about coupled preparation and local output.

6. What would falsify H1?

If carefully controlled experiments show that a local effective state always contains all information needed for the relevant prediction, and enlarging the receiver provides no transferable predictive gain, then the distinctive receiver claim loses its purpose.

This is the deepest failure because H2 and H3 were introduced as possible structures inside the larger receiver picture.

7. What survives independently of all three?

Several methodological distinctions remain useful even if the Titraj hypotheses fail:

  • measurement, mathematical formalism and physical mechanism should not be silently identified;
  • a discrete detector output does not by itself reveal the complete propagation history;
  • source, field, receiver and readout are different physical stages;
  • any proposed ontology must reproduce the same quantitative observations.

These are disciplines of interpretation, not dependent predictions of H1–H3.

Deep Notes

This section treats Titraj as a nested research programme rather than one all-or-nothing theory. It states what each layer claims, which equations belong to which layer, and what should be deleted if a layer is falsified.

1. Why hypothesis separation matters

Speculative models become difficult to test when every failed detail can be absorbed into a more general story. The cure is to label commitments before the experiment.

Path III therefore distinguishes:

\[H_1:\ \text{organised receiver},\]
\[H_2:\ \text{frequency-selected geometry or mode},\]
\[H_3:\ \text{constrained local-}c\text{ trajectory}.\]

H2 presupposes that some organised receiver state exists, while H3 adds a specific microscopic kinematic rule. The logical dependencies must not be reversed after a failed test.

2. H1 has no local-c requirement

The minimal H1 model can be written schematically as

\[R(t)=F\bigl(R_0,E(t),B(t),G,\text{environment}\bigr),\]

followed by an output rule

\[P(O)=\mathcal G[R(t)].\]

The state \(R(t)\) may be classical, semiclassical, quantum or hybrid depending on the problem. H1 only asks whether relevant predictive information is lost when the receiver is compressed too early to one local carrier.

3. H2 needs a measurable frequency-to-mode map

H2 becomes scientific only when it specifies a mapping such as

\[\nu\mapsto \{M_j,w_j(\nu)\},\]

where \(M_j\) are receiver modes and \(w_j\) their frequency-dependent weights or access probabilities.

The prediction may concern resonance, spatial pattern, phase relation, harmonic response, polarisation dependence or momentum distribution. The mode need not have the geometry of a classical trajectory.

4. H3 owns the half-cycle arc-length equation

Only H3 asserts

\[|\dot{\mathbf r}|=c.\]

Therefore only H3 is entitled to infer

\[s_{1/2}=\frac{c}{2f}.\]

If H3 is removed, \(\lambda/2\) may remain relevant to antenna resonance, standing-wave geometry, electrical length or another mode scale, but it can no longer be presented as the distance one electron necessarily travels during half a cycle.

5. The local-c path must make extra predictions

H3 is not justified merely because a curved path can be drawn with the desired arc length. It must produce observations that H1 and H2 do not already produce.

Examples would include a distinctive bound on output momentum, a specific polarisation law, a harmonic signature, a timing relation or a geometry-dependent effect that follows from \(|\dot{\mathbf r}|=c\) itself.

If every successful prediction can be reproduced after deleting the local-c condition, then the condition is empirically idle.

6. Falsification should propagate downward only where dependence is real

If H3 fails, delete H3-dependent statements. If H2 fails, delete its frequency-geometry rules and H3 if H3 depended on them. If H1 fails, the distinctive Titraj receiver programme fails.

The reverse propagation is not valid. Failure of H3 does not logically prove that a one-electron receiver was sufficient all along.

7. A possible outcome is partial survival

A mature result could be much less dramatic than the original conjecture. For example:

local-c path rejected ↓ frequency-selected geometry weakened or revised ↓ organised receiver retained only where it improves prediction

That would still be a scientifically useful outcome because it identifies exactly which layer carried the explanatory value.

8. Methodological claims are not substitute physics

Separating observation from ontology is useful, but it does not by itself explain photoemission, spontaneous emission, Compton scattering or nonclassical correlations.

Those phenomena still require quantitative models. The methodological distinction survives a failed Titraj theory, but it cannot be presented as though it were a replacement theory.

9. The preferred rule

\[\boxed{\text{When a strong conjecture fails, remove it and every result that uniquely depended on it.}}\]

This rule keeps Path III falsifiable and prevents the word Titraj from expanding to mean whatever remains unexplained.

10. What this theme establishes

The core receiver hypothesis, the frequency-selected geometry and the local-c path are not one claim. Their predictions and failure conditions can be tested separately.

That separation allows the research programme to become narrower without becoming evasive.
