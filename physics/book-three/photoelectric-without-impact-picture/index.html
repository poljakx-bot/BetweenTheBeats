Path III · Theme 4

The Photoelectric Effect Without the Impact Picture

The measurements remain exact. The question is whether discreteness must be assigned to a localised travelling object before reception.

Published essay

Four questions to keep separate

Measurement is not the same thing as its explanation.

As you read, keep four tasks distinct: the standard account; what the measurement directly establishes; the portal’s objection or alternative; and the evidence that could distinguish the two.

Intuition

The familiar story is efficient.

One photon carries energy \(h\nu\).

It strikes one electron.

The electron spends \(\phi\) escaping and leaves with

\[K_{\max}=h\nu-\phi.\]

The equation is correct.

The measurements are precise.

The statistical quantum theory describing the outcomes is extraordinarily successful.

But the experiment and the mechanical picture are not the same thing.

The apparatus observes:

  • a prepared electromagnetic input;
  • a material surface in a defined state;
  • an emitted-electron distribution;
  • a stopping potential or reconstructed kinetic energy;
  • frequency, intensity, timing, polarisation and geometry dependence.

It does not observe a small object travelling from the source, choosing one electron and colliding with it.

The sharper Titraj question is therefore:

Does the photoelectric effect prove that the discreteness existed as a localised travelling object before reception, or does it prove that a frequency-governed interaction ends in a discrete material transfer?

The portal does not challenge the discreteness of the completed event.

It challenges the automatic relocation of that discreteness into the propagation history.

The Argument

The measured upper edge obeys

\[K_{\max}=h\nu-\phi,\]

or

\[eV_s=h\nu-\phi.\]

The threshold frequency is

\[\nu_0=\frac{\phi}{h}.\]

The observations establish that:

  • ordinary single-event photoemission has a material-dependent threshold;
  • the maximum electron energy depends linearly on frequency;
  • intensity mainly changes the event rate in the linear regime;
  • no classically expected accumulation delay appears;
  • electron direction, probability and energy distribution depend on material, surface and polarisation.

These facts defeated the naive model of one isolated electron slowly collecting continuous classical energy like a bucket.

They did not directly photograph the textbook transaction.

Observation, formalism and ontology

Three layers must remain separate.

Observation:

\[K_{\max}=h\nu-\phi.\]

Quantum formalism: permitted transitions and outcome probabilities are calculated with a universal energy scale \(h\nu\).

Ontology: a discrete object containing \(h\nu\) travelled through space and struck one electron.

The first two are experimentally indispensable.

The third is an interpretation of what the formalism represents physically.

Modern quantum field theory is more subtle than the pellet cartoon, but popular explanations often move from “one excitation” back to “one little object on one path” without marking the change.

Where may the discreteness reside?

The standard account places the elementary step in the field:

\[E_\gamma=h\nu.\]

Titraj tests another placement:

\[E_f-E_i=h\nu\]

may identify one permitted reorganisation of the coupled material system, while the field between source and receiver remains electromagnetic and spatially extended.

The alternatives can be stated as:

\[\text{discrete field object} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{discrete material event},\]

or

\[\text{electromagnetic field} \\ + \\ \text{discrete organised receiver} \\ \rightarrow \\ \text{discrete completed event}.\]

The photoelectric equation alone does not uniquely locate the discreteness.

Frequency selects the energy relation; intensity changes the rate

A receiver has permitted initial and final states. A field of frequency \(\nu\) couples efficiently when the relevant energy difference satisfies

\[E_f-E_i=h\nu.\]

For an escaping final state, the energy accounting includes the surface barrier and any losses. The maximum-energy edge gives the familiar relation.

At fixed \(\nu\), increasing field intensity changes the transition probability or event rate. It need not change the energy difference defining the final state.

Thus the observed division

\[\nu\rightarrow\text{energy scale},\]
\[I\rightarrow\text{event rate}\]

can be described as a property of the coupled field–receiver dynamics.

It does not, by itself, prove that intensity is literally a count of independent pellets in transit.

The receiver creates the local record

One electron eventually leaves.

One detector pulse is accepted.

One count is stored.

That local discrete outcome is real.

But the apparatus does not track an individually labelled electron from the beginning of preparation to the output. Nor does the click reveal the complete spatial form of the incoming influence before completion.

The canonical distinction is:

\[\text{one completed elementary transfer} \\ \not\Rightarrow \\ \text{one independently identifiable object carried the whole history}.\]

The source side cannot be ignored

Receiver dynamics cannot manufacture arbitrary source correlations.

Different prepared source states produce different count statistics in the same detector. Antibunching, bunching and coherent-source statistics constrain the full source–field–receiver model.

Therefore Titraj does not reduce the field to an empty messenger whose properties are invented at detection.

It argues only that the detector event is not a direct photograph of a localised trajectory.

The propagating state must preserve the experimentally observed correlations, even if its ontology is not a classical particle path.

Deep Notes

1. Semiclassical models explain more than the popular story suggests

A quantised material system driven by a classical electromagnetic field can reproduce many features of absorption and stimulated transitions.

That fact weakens the claim that the elementary photoelectric relation alone proves a particle in transit.

It does not prove that field quantisation is unnecessary in all phenomena.

2. Antibunching is a serious boundary

An ordinary positive classical stochastic field combined with independent local detection cannot reproduce all corrected antibunching results.

This is evidence for nonclassical source–field structure.

It is not an image of a tiny object choosing a path.

Titraj must preserve the first statement without converting it into the second.

3. A non-clicking branch need not be physically empty

Real detectors contain analogue response, noise, leakage and subthreshold structure. One accepted event in one branch does not prove that nothing physical occurred in the other receiver.

But subthreshold response also does not automatically carry the energy signature of a second completed photoelectron. Apparatus-specific traces cannot be promoted into a universal explanation.

4. Compton scattering is a separate and stronger obligation

Compton experiments impose event-wise energy–momentum correlations.

A receiver-only account cannot create those constraints after arrival. Any alternative field ontology must reproduce them quantitatively.

The successful impulse or one-electron kinematics need not imply a hidden pellet trajectory, but the kinematic constraints are real and non-negotiable.

5. Photon has several meanings

The word may refer to:

  • the energy unit \(h\nu\);
  • a quantum excitation of a field mode;
  • a number-state label;
  • a detected elementary transfer;
  • a localised projectile in a popular picture.

The portal challenges the silent movement from the first four meanings to the fifth.

6. The revised claim

The strongest defensible portal statement is:

The photoelectric effect demonstrates frequency-governed discrete energy exchange and discrete electron output. By itself, it does not uniquely establish that a localised photon travelled from source to receiver carrying the complete causal history.

The next theme examines the two material boundaries together: generation and reception.
