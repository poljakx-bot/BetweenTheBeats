Path III · Theme 10

What Would a Constrained-Momentum Model Have to Predict?

A geometry is not yet a mechanism. If frequency selects an organised receiver mode, that mode must predict the momentum distribution of the final output before the detector measures it.

Published essay

From picture to prediction

The model must end where the experiment begins.

Photoemission experiments can resolve electron energy, momentum direction, angular distribution and their dependence on polarisation and target orientation. A constrained-momentum model earns relevance only if it predicts those observables quantitatively.

Intuition

The detector does not see the proposed internal path

A detector does not report that an electron followed a curved half-cycle path inside matter. It reports a final event: an electron arrived with some energy and some momentum direction.

Therefore the proposed internal geometry cannot be judged by how plausible it looks. It must generate the same output variables that the apparatus measures.

\[\boxed{\text{receiver geometry}\rightarrow\text{predicted momentum distribution}}\]

Frequency, intensity and polarisation must do different jobs

The Path III hypothesis gives frequency the role of selecting an allowed dynamical mode or geometry. Intensity mainly controls how strongly or how often that mode is driven in the linear regime. Polarisation supplies directional information to the receiver.

If those roles are physically real, changing them separately should produce separately predictable changes in the output.

The model must therefore answer questions such as:

  • What is the maximum output momentum at this frequency?
  • Which directions are favoured or forbidden?
  • How does rotating linear polarisation rotate or reshape the distribution?
  • What changes when the receiver itself is rotated?
  • Which part of the distribution changes with intensity before nonlinear channels appear?

A model that cannot answer these questions has not yet connected its internal picture to the experiment.

The Argument

1. Define the observable before the mechanism

Let the final emitted electron have momentum vector

\[\mathbf p=(p_x,p_y,p_z).\]

The experiment may measure an energy spectrum, an angular distribution, or a full momentum map. The most useful predictive object is therefore not one preferred trajectory but a distribution

\[P\!\left(\mathbf p\mid \nu,I,\boldsymbol\epsilon,\mathbf k,R_0,G\right),\]

where \(\nu\) is drive frequency, \(I\) intensity, \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) polarisation, \(\mathbf k\) propagation direction, \(R_0\) the prepared receiver state and \(G\) its geometry.

2. The magnitude of momentum is constrained by the measured energy

In ordinary non-relativistic photoemission, kinetic energy and momentum magnitude are related by

\[K=\frac{p^2}{2m_e}.\]

For the maximum-energy edge, the measured stopping-potential relation is commonly written

\[eV_s=h\nu-\phi.\]

Whatever ontology is assigned to the incoming radiation, a successful alternative must reproduce the measured edge. Thus it must also reproduce the corresponding maximum momentum

\[p_{\max}=\sqrt{2m_e eV_s}=\sqrt{2m_e(h\nu-\phi)}\]

within the regime where the non-relativistic relation applies.

The portal does not take this equation as proof of a projectile carrying \(h\nu\). It takes it as a numerical boundary that the receiver model must recover.

3. Direction contains information that the energy edge does not

Two electrons can leave with the same kinetic energy and different directions. Therefore a scalar threshold or energy balance cannot determine the full event.

The model must predict

\[\frac{dP}{d\Omega}\]

or, more strongly, the full momentum distribution. Photoionization experiments show that these distributions change with the prepared state, target structure and polarisation of the driving field. A physical receiver geometry must reproduce that directional sensitivity rather than add it afterwards.

4. Polarisation is a direct geometric test

Hold frequency and intensity fixed and rotate the incident polarisation. If the receiver has a constrained internal geometry, the coupling to that geometry changes.

The model should therefore predict in advance how

\[P(\mathbf p)\rightarrow P'(\mathbf p)\]

when \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) is rotated, made elliptical or made circular.

Experiments already use polarisation-dependent photoelectron angular distributions to recover detailed ionisation information. That makes polarisation a stringent constraint on any proposed Titraj geometry, not a secondary visual feature.

5. Receiver orientation separates field geometry from material geometry

A second control is to keep the field fixed and rotate the target or organised receiver.

If the output changes, the model must decide whether the change came from:

\[\text{field orientation relative to the receiver},\]

from a changed receiver state, or from a later escape/scattering process.

The important comparison is between experiments that preserve local energy conditions while changing only geometry.

6. Momentum conservation cannot be postponed to the detector

The emitted electron is not the only possible carrier of momentum. Residual matter, recoil, emitted or scattered fields and other degrees of freedom can participate. A complete event must satisfy a balance of the form

\[\mathbf P_{\rm initial}=\mathbf p_e+\mathbf P_{\rm residual}+\mathbf P_{\rm other}.\]

If Path III rejects a literal photon projectile, it does not gain permission to ignore electromagnetic momentum. The incoming field has momentum flow, and the proposed material response must show how that momentum is redistributed into the observed final channels.

7. Separate the three Titraj hypotheses

H1 — organised many-electron receiver: predicts that the output distribution depends on receiver state beyond the final local electron.

H2 — frequency-selected geometry: predicts that changing \(\nu\) changes which internal response geometry is available and therefore changes the allowed momentum distribution in a structured way.

H3 — constrained local-\(c\) path: adds a much stronger claim. A specified internal path \(\mathbf r(t)\) must map into a calculable output momentum and should produce extra restrictions not already implied by H1 or H2.

If H3 produces no new prediction, it is unnecessary. If H3 fails while H1 or H2 survive, the broader receiver program need not fail with it.

8. The required chain

\[\boxed{\nu,I,\boldsymbol\epsilon,R_0,G\rightarrow\text{internal response}\rightarrow P(\mathbf p)\rightarrow\text{measured output}.}\]

This chain must be evaluated forward. Choosing a path only after the measured angular pattern is known would not test the model.

Deep Notes

This section begins with the measurable final electron and works backwards only far enough to define what a predictive internal model must supply. It does not assume that the internal Titraj geometry is already correct.

1. What a momentum-resolved experiment supplies

An emitted electron can be characterised by kinetic energy and direction. In a non-relativistic regime,

\[K=\frac{p^2}{2m_e},\qquad \mathbf p=p\,\hat{\mathbf n}.\]

A momentum-resolved experiment therefore gives information that a stopping-potential measurement alone does not: the direction \(\hat{\mathbf n}\), the width of the distribution, asymmetries, nodal directions and correlations with the driving field.

Modern photoionisation experiments deliberately vary polarisation and reconstruct two- or three-dimensional photoelectron distributions. Those measurements demonstrate that the momentum pattern carries detailed information about both the incoming field and the prepared target state.

2. The prediction should be a distribution, not a cartoon trajectory

A microscopic model need not predict the exact momentum of every individual event deterministically. It may predict a probability distribution:

\[P\!\left(\mathbf p\mid \nu,I,\boldsymbol\epsilon,\mathbf k,R_0,G\right).\]

This formulation is deliberately compatible with statistical quantum outcomes. The question is whether a proposed physical receiver geometry can generate the correct distribution and its changes under controlled perturbations.

3. Frequency must predict more than threshold

The measured photoelectric edge supplies one scalar constraint:

\[eV_s=h\nu-\phi.\]

For a non-relativistic electron this corresponds to

\[p_{\max}(\nu)=\sqrt{2m_e(h\nu-\phi)}.\]

A constrained-momentum model must recover this relation where it applies, but that is only the beginning. It must also predict how the shape of \(P(\mathbf p)\) evolves with frequency.

If H2 says that frequency selects geometry, then neighbouring frequencies should not merely rescale one arbitrary distribution. They should alter it according to a derived mode-selection rule.

4. Intensity must be separated from mode selection

In the weak or linear regime, Path III assigns intensity mainly to response magnitude, participation or event probability. A simple expectation is therefore that changing \(I\) at fixed \(\nu\) initially changes the normalisation or rate more strongly than the frequency-defined momentum boundary.

At higher intensities, multiphoton, strong-field and other nonlinear processes can reshape the distribution. The model must specify where its simple frequency/intensity separation ceases to apply rather than treating every deviation as a new Titraj mode.

5. Polarisation supplies a directional input

Let \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) describe the electric-field polarisation. The predicted distribution must transform when \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) changes.

This creates a clean test because frequency and material can be held fixed while linear polarisation is rotated or changed to elliptical or circular polarisation. Photoelectron angular-distribution experiments show strong and measurable polarisation dependence, so a physical geometry must reproduce both allowed directions and suppressed directions.

6. Material orientation supplies an independent directional input

If an anisotropic receiver is rotated while the field is fixed, the relative geometry changes. The model should predict the transformed distribution from the same internal rules.

This distinguishes a receiver geometry from a theory that merely aligns the final electron with the external field by assumption.

7. Field momentum remains part of the accounting

A continuous electromagnetic field carries energy and momentum. In vacuum, the Poynting vector \(\mathbf S\) describes energy flux and the associated field momentum density can be written

\[\mathbf g=\frac{\mathbf S}{c^2}.\]

Therefore rejecting a localised photon path does not remove momentum from the incoming field. The receiver model must provide a dynamical route by which field momentum and receiver momentum are redistributed into the emitted electron, recoil and any remaining field.

8. H1, H2 and H3 make different commitments

H1 can be tested by changing distributed receiver preparation while matching local output conditions. H2 can be tested by asking whether frequency-dependent momentum maps follow a derived family of receiver modes. H3 must go further and calculate a relation between the proposed local-\(c\) path and the final momentum distribution.

For H3, a complete formulation would need at least:

\[\mathbf r(t;\nu,R_0,G,\boldsymbol\epsilon),\]

a rule connecting the internal constrained motion to momentum transfer, and a rule for when the bound response becomes an escaping degree of freedom.

Only then can the path predict rather than illustrate the measured output.

9. A practical falsification programme

A useful experimental programme would vary one control at a time while predicting the full momentum map before measurement:

  • rotate linear polarisation at fixed frequency and intensity;
  • switch between linear, elliptical and circular polarisation;
  • rotate an anisotropic receiver while holding the field fixed;
  • sweep frequency through a threshold or resonance while keeping the receiver preparation controlled;
  • vary intensity within the linear regime before entering known nonlinear channels;
  • alter distributed receiver geometry while preserving the same local surface and nominal escape barrier.

The model should predict not only whether emission occurs, but the changes in peak momentum, width, asymmetry, nodal directions and angular weights.

10. Failure conditions

The constrained-momentum programme fails if its geometry can be chosen only after seeing the momentum map, if it predicts no more than energy conservation already supplies, or if every polarisation and orientation result requires a new apparatus-specific path.

A stronger failure would occur if a fixed proposed path family predicts angular or momentum restrictions that are systematically absent in controlled experiments.

11. What this theme establishes

\[\boxed{\text{a physical internal geometry must predict }P(\mathbf p),\text{ not merely resemble it}.}\]

This is the point at which the Titraj programme can move from a qualitative mechanism to a quantitative research problem.
