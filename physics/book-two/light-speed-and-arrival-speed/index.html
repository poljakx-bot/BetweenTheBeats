Light Speed Is Not Arrival Speed

A travelling light front can propagate at c while its separation from a moving receiver closes at c±v. These are different rates defined in the same coordinate frame.

Published essay

Three quantities to keep separate

The signal, the receiver and their meeting are not one trajectory.

Keep distinct the propagation speed of the travelling light feature, the coordinate closing rate between that feature and a receiver, and the local light-speed measurement made in the receiver’s own inertial frame.

Intuition

The front and the meeting have different rates

A light signal and its arrival at a particular receiver are not the same physical quantity. In a chosen laboratory frame, a marked light front can move at \(c\). A receiver may simultaneously move towards that front or away from it.

If the receiver moves towards the incoming front, the remaining separation disappears faster. If it moves away, the separation disappears more slowly.

\[\boxed{\text{propagation speed}\neq\text{closing rate}}\]

This does not require the light front itself to acquire the receiver’s velocity. It is simply the geometry of two trajectories approaching a common reception event.

The event does not travel

A lightning strike, switch closure or clock mark happens at the source. What later reaches another location is an electromagnetic record of that event.

source event ↓ travelling electromagnetic record ↓ reception event

Confusing those three stages makes later discussions of clocks and simultaneity much harder than they need to be.

The Argument

1. Choose one coordinate frame

Let a marked light front move to the right in a specified external frame:

\[x_F(t)=x_0+ct.\]

In that frame, its propagation speed is

\[\frac{dx_F}{dt}=c.\]

2. Add a moving receiver

Let the receiver move along the same axis:

\[x_R(t)=x_{R0}+vt.\]

Reception occurs when the two worldlines meet:

\[x_F(t_{rec})=x_R(t_{rec}).\]

3. Follow their separation instead of the front

Define the coordinate separation

\[D(t)=x_R(t)-x_F(t).\]

Then

\[\frac{dD}{dt}=v-c.\]

For a receiver receding in the same direction as the front, the magnitude of the closing rate is

\[v_{close}=c-v.\]

For a receiver moving towards an incoming front, it is

\[v_{close}=c+v.\]

Thus, within the same coordinate description,

\[\boxed{v_{front}=c},\qquad \boxed{v_{close}=c\pm v}.\]

4. What these equations do not say

The quantity \(c\pm v\) is not being claimed as the locally measured speed of light in the receiver’s inertial frame. In standard special relativity, an inertial receiver measuring light locally obtains \(c\). The quantity above is a coordinate closing rate between two worldlines described in one chosen frame.

5. Why the distinction matters

Receiver motion changes when the reception event occurs even when the chosen-frame trajectory of the light front remains unchanged. Therefore a statement about “how fast the information arrives at this moving receiver” must specify whether it means front propagation, worldline closing rate or a local light-speed measurement.

Deep Notes

Define the physical problem from the beginning

Consider a source that creates a distinguishable electromagnetic mark: a pulse edge, phase mark or other identifiable feature. The source event occurs once. The mark then propagates. A receiver later intersects that travelling feature.

There are therefore three different objects in the description:

source event ↓ light-front worldline ↓ receiver worldline and reception event

The rate attached to the light-front worldline and the rate attached to the shrinking separation between the two worldlines need not be the same quantity.

Propagation speed in a chosen frame

In one inertial coordinate system, write the front as

\[x_F(t)=x_F(0)+ct.\]

Differentiating gives

\[v_F=\frac{dx_F}{dt}=c.\]

This rate follows one travelling feature through the coordinate system.

Closing rate to a moving receiver

Let the receiver trajectory be

\[x_R(t)=x_R(0)+vt.\]

The separation is

\[D(t)=x_R(t)-x_F(t).\]

Therefore

\[\dot D=v-c.\]

If the receiver recedes in the same direction as the front, the positive closing magnitude is \(c-v\). If the receiver moves towards the incoming front, the separation can close at \(c+v\).

A closing rate larger than \(c\) does not mean that a signal travels locally at more than \(c\). It means that two already defined trajectories converge at that coordinate rate.

A simple geometric analogy

Two objects moving towards one another at ordinary subluminal speeds can have a separation that shrinks faster than either object moves. The derivative of separation is a property of the pair of trajectories, not a new object travelling between them. The light-front calculation is the same kind of distinction.

A sequence of emitted marks

Suppose a source emits two distinguishable marks separated by source-frame coordinate time \(T\). In that same frame their spatial separation after both are in flight is

\[\Delta x=cT.\]

For a receiver moving towards the sequence with speed \(v\) in that coordinate frame, the interval between encounters is

\[\Delta t_{rec}=\frac{\Delta x}{c+v}=\frac{T}{1+v/c}.\]

For a receiver receding from the sequence,

\[\Delta t_{rec}=\frac{\Delta x}{c-v}=\frac{T}{1-v/c}.\]

These expressions describe encounter timing in the selected external coordinates. They show directly that receiver motion changes the cadence at which travelling marks are met.

Why this is not yet a statement about the receiver’s clock

The coordinate intervals above are not automatically the proper-time intervals recorded by an arbitrary moving clock. Converting between coordinate time and the receiver’s proper time introduces the spacetime transformation appropriate to the chosen theory. Keeping that later step separate prevents an encounter calculation from being mistaken for a complete clock comparison.

Retarded time and source history

A received electromagnetic field represents an earlier source state. In the simplest fixed-distance case,

\[t_{ret}=t_{rec}-\frac{R}{c}.\]

The equation identifies which earlier source state is represented in the field received now. It does not make the earlier source event travel to the receiver. The event remains in the source history; the electromagnetic record propagates.

Standard SR statement and the portal distinction

Standard special relativity states that every inertial observer who performs a local measurement of light speed obtains \(c\). Nothing on this page requires a different local measurement.

The portal’s narrower distinction is that another rate exists simultaneously in any chosen coordinate description:

\[\text{closing rate of front and receiver}=c\pm v.\]

Calling both quantities simply “the speed at which light reaches the observer” hides which derivative is actually being discussed.

Terminology used in Path II

Propagation speed follows the travelling electromagnetic feature in a specified frame.

Closing rate follows the changing coordinate separation between that feature and a receiver.

Reception event is their local intersection.

Local measured light speed is the result of a local operational measurement by an observer; in standard SR it is \(c\).

Source event remains part of the source history and is represented later only through the travelling signal.

What this theme establishes

Receiver motion can change the reception time and encounter cadence without changing the chosen-frame propagation speed of the light front. This is a purely kinematic distinction.

What it does not yet establish is any alternative transformation law for clocks, simultaneity or proper time. Those questions require additional assumptions and are treated separately.

Connection to the novel

Jan’s correction begins with vocabulary. A signal can travel, an event cannot, and the rate at which a receiver meets the signal is not automatically the propagation speed assigned to that signal.

The distinction becomes the foundation for every later question about clocks, frames and reports.
