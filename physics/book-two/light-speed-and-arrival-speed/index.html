Path II · Theme 1

Light Speed Is Not Arrival Speed

The wave, the message to a chosen receiver, and the receiver’s local measurement are not the same physical quantity.

Published essay

Three quantities to keep separate

Wave speed, message speed and local measurement answer different questions.

This path uses those terms deliberately. A wave can propagate with one defined rate, a moving receiver can meet the carried message at another rate, and a local measurement can reconstruct a third operational quantity without changing the history already travelled by the signal.

Intuition

A wave is not the same thing as a message to one receiver

Imagine a source emits a marked pulse. The pulse propagates. A receiver later meets it.

source event ↓ travelling wave record ↓ message received by a chosen receiver

The wave has a propagation history whether the receiver moves or not. Moving the receiver changes where and when the meeting occurs. It does not reach backwards and rewrite the route already travelled by the wave.

This gives three questions that should not be compressed into one phrase such as “the speed of light”:

\[\boxed{\text{wave propagation}}\qquad\boxed{\text{message arrival}}\qquad\boxed{\text{local measurement}}\]

The receiver changes the message path, not the earlier wave history

In a chosen external frame, let the marked light front propagate at \(c\). If the receiver moves away at speed \(v\), the remaining distance closes at \(c-v\). If the receiver moves towards the front, it closes at \(c+v\).

The portal calls this receiver-dependent transport quantity the message or arrival rate. It belongs to the source–receiver pair. It is not a claim that the wave itself changed its propagation rate to \(c\pm v\).

The wave carries the record. The receiver determines where that record is met.

Why local measurement is a separate question

A receiver can also perform a local measurement using its own clocks, rulers, frequency and wavelength reconstruction. That operational result need not be identified in advance with the source-to-receiver arrival rate. Path II asks what happens when those two questions are kept separate instead of being given the same name.

The Argument

1. Define the wave trajectory

Choose one coordinate frame in which a marked electromagnetic feature leaves the source at \(t=0\) and propagates to the right:

\[x_M(t)=ct.\]

The rate attached to that travelling wave record is therefore

\[\frac{dx_M}{dt}=c.\]

2. Define a moving receiver

Let the receiver initially be a distance \(L\) ahead and move in the same direction at speed \(v\):

\[x_R(t)=L+vt.\]

The message arrives when

\[x_M(t_{arr})=x_R(t_{arr}).\]

Therefore

\[ct_{arr}=L+vt_{arr},\]
\[\boxed{t_{arr}=\frac{L}{c-v}}.\]

3. Define message speed operationally

If “message speed to this receiver” means the initial source–receiver separation divided by the measured arrival time, then

\[v_{msg}\equiv\frac{L}{t_{arr}}=c-v.\]

For a receiver moving towards the incoming message, the analogous geometry gives

\[\boxed{v_{msg}=c+v}.\]

This does not say that the electromagnetic wave has changed its propagation rate. It says that a particular message has a receiver-dependent arrival history.

4. The same distinction exists for sound

For sound propagating through a medium at \(c_s\), with a receiver receding at \(u\), the same equations give

\[t_{arr}=\frac{L}{c_s-u},\qquad v_{msg}=c_s-u.\]

No one needs to conclude that the sound wave itself now propagates through the medium at \(c_s-u\). The quantity belongs to the encounter with that receiver.

5. Where special relativity adds another step

Standard special relativity states that an inertial observer performing a local light-speed measurement obtains \(c\). This page does not deny that operational statement. It asks whether the local result and the source-to-moving-receiver message rate are the same physical question.

The portal keeps them separate:

\[\boxed{v_{wave}=c}\]
\[\boxed{v_{msg}=c\pm v}\]
\[\boxed{v_{local}=c\quad\text{in the standard SR measurement}}\]

The disagreement examined later in Path II begins when the second and third quantities are treated as though one automatically defines the other.

Deep Notes

Start from the physical events

A source event occurs at \(A\). It produces an identifiable electromagnetic disturbance. A receiver later detects that disturbance at event \(B\).

A: emission ↓ propagating electromagnetic record ↓ B: reception

The original event does not travel. The field record does. The message is the causal connection from the source event to the chosen reception event.

Three rates can therefore enter the description and should be defined before any transformation is applied.

Wave propagation rate

In a selected emission-frame coordinate description, let the travelling record obey

\[x_M(t)=x_A+ct.\]

The derivative

\[v_{wave}=\frac{dx_M}{dt}=c\]

belongs to the wave trajectory in that coordinate description. It says nothing yet about where a moving receiver will intersect that trajectory.

Receiver trajectory and arrival event

Let a receiver move according to

\[x_R(t)=x_R(0)+vt.\]

The reception event is the intersection

\[x_M(t_{arr})=x_R(t_{arr}).\]

For an initial separation \(L=x_R(0)-x_A\) and a receiver receding in the direction of propagation,

\[(c-v)t_{arr}=L.\]

Hence

\[t_{arr}=\frac{L}{c-v}.\]

If the receiver approaches the incoming record, the corresponding denominator is \(c+v\).

What “message speed” means on this portal

Path II reserves message speed or arrival speed for the operational ratio

\[v_{msg}=\frac{\text{initial source–receiver separation}}{\text{time until that receiver receives the message}}.\]

With the geometry above,

\[\boxed{v_{msg}=c-v}\]

for recession and

\[\boxed{v_{msg}=c+v}\]

for approach.

This terminology is intentionally receiver-specific. If the receiver trajectory changes after emission, the arrival event changes even though the earlier wave trajectory remains the same.

A receiver that changes speed after emission

Suppose the message still follows

\[x_M(t)=ct,\]

while the receiver moves first with \(v_1\) and later with \(v_2\):

\[x_R(t)=\begin{cases}L+v_1t,&t<t_1,\\x_R(t_1)+v_2(t-t_1),&t\ge t_1.\end{cases}\]

The receiver has changed the location of the future intersection. Nothing in this construction requires the already propagating message to alter the portion of its history before \(t_1\).

This is why the portal treats message arrival as a property of two intersecting histories rather than as a new intrinsic wave speed.

The sound analogue

Sound makes the distinction visible without relativistic coordinate transformations. Let the medium define a frame in which a marked sound disturbance propagates at \(c_s\):

\[x_M(t)=c_st.\]

A receiver a distance \(L\) ahead recedes at \(u\):

\[x_R(t)=L+ut.\]

The arrival condition gives

\[t_{arr}=\frac{L}{c_s-u}.\]

The wave still propagates through the medium at \(c_s\), while the message to that receiver arrives according to \(c_s-u\). The two statements coexist because they describe different relations.

Local measurement is a third construction

A receiver can estimate a wave speed from quantities available in its own measurement procedure: elapsed local time, spatial separation between local detectors, frequency, wavelength or phase. Which operational definition is used matters.

In standard special relativity, every inertial local light-speed measurement gives

\[v_{local}=c.\]

The portal does not replace that empirical statement with \(c\pm v\). Instead it asks whether the quantity measured locally must also be identified with the source-to-receiver message rate defined by the emission and reception events.

Where the interpretive disagreement begins

Special relativity uses Lorentz coordinates and Einstein synchronization so that the one-way coordinate speed of light is \(c\) in every inertial frame. Within that framework, the receiver’s coordinates describe light locally with the same invariant speed.

Path II asks a different methodological question: when a message was emitted before the receiver’s later motion or synchronization convention is applied, should the physical history of that message be reconstructed from the receiver’s coordinate convention, or should the propagation history and the encounter with the receiver remain separate objects?

The distinction can be summarised as

\[\boxed{\text{wave history}\neq\text{receiver-dependent arrival history}\neq\text{local measurement convention}}\]

What is and is not established here

The geometry establishes that receiver motion changes the arrival event and therefore the ratio \(L/t_{arr}\). It also establishes that this can be described without changing the chosen-frame trajectory of the travelling wave record.

It does not by itself derive an alternative spacetime transformation, invalidate measured local values of \(c\), or settle clock synchronization. Those later questions require their own argument.

Connection to the novel

Jan’s correction begins with vocabulary. The wave carries a record, the message ends at a chosen receiver, and the local measurement performed after reception is another operation.

Mixing those three rates is the conceptual knot that the rest of Path II tries to untie.
