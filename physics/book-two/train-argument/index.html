What the Train Argument Actually Establishes

The train becomes important when the same messenger speed is imposed in both inertial descriptions, forcing a transformation of distant time.

Published essay

Intuition

The train argument is often introduced through a passenger who receives one flash before another. That reception order is ordinary encounter geometry: the passenger moves towards one signal and away from the other while both are travelling.

The non-trivial step is different. An observer outside a moving laboratory describes a wave crossing endpoints that continue to move. An observer inside the laboratory uses a spatial grid and distant clocks arranged so that the same messenger has equal measured speed in both directions. The two descriptions can agree on the events while assigning different coordinate times to spatially separated points.

Once a signal speed \(w\) is required to have the same value in every inertial coordinate system, a Galilean transformation cannot preserve it. The time coordinate must acquire a position-dependent correction. That requirement leads to a Lorentz-form transformation.

The same reasoning can be applied to sound. If sound is taken to retain the invariant measured value \(c_s\), the resulting transformation has the same form with \(c_s\) in place of \(c\). This reveals the question examined here: does the limiting speed describe a universal boundary of nature, or the boundary of a coordinate construction built around the chosen messenger?

The Argument

1. Begin with an unspecified invariant signal speed

Let inertial system \(S'\) move at speed \(v\) relative to \(S\). Suppose a signal has speed \(w\) in both directions in \(S\), so its fronts satisfy

\[x=wt\qquad\text{and}\qquad x=-wt.\]

Assume a linear transformation because uniform motion should remain uniform:

\[x'=a(x-vt),\qquad t'=b(t-\kappa x).\]

The coefficient \(\kappa\) permits the time assigned to an event to depend on its position. Galilean time would correspond to \(\kappa=0\).

2. Preserve the right-moving front

For \(x=wt\), require the transformed front to satisfy \(x'=wt'\). Substitution gives

\[a(w-v)=wb(1-\kappa w).\]

3. Preserve the left-moving front

For \(x=-wt\), require \(x'=-wt'\). This gives

\[a(w+v)=wb(1+\kappa w).\]

4. Solve the directional conditions

Adding and subtracting the two relations yields

\[a=b,\qquad \kappa=\frac{v}{w^2}.\]

Reciprocity between the two inertial systems fixes the common scale factor:

\[a=b=\gamma_w=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/w^2}}.\]

The required transformation is therefore

\[\boxed{x'=\gamma_w(x-vt)},\qquad \boxed{t'=\gamma_w\left(t-\frac{vx}{w^2}\right)}.\]

5. Why time had to change

If only space transformed, \(x'=x-vt\) and \(t'=t\), the right-moving signal would have speed \(w-v\) in \(S'\). Preserving \(w\) requires the denominator used to calculate speed to change as well. The term \(-vx/w^2\) changes the assigned times of spatially separated events and produces relativity of simultaneity.

6. Light and sound as two substitutions

For light, set \(w=c\):

\[x'=\gamma(x-vt),\qquad t'=\gamma\left(t-\frac{vx}{c^2}\right).\]

For sound, suppose a moving receiver measures changed frequency and perceived wavelength whose product remains the sound propagation speed:

\[f'_s\lambda'_s=c_s.\]

If that same measured value is required in every inertial coordinate system, set \(w=c_s\):

\[x'_s=\gamma_s(x-vt),\qquad t'_s=\gamma_s\left(t-\frac{vx}{c_s^2}\right),\] \[\gamma_s=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c_s^2}}.\]

For \(x=\pm c_st\), substitution gives \(x'_s/t'_s=\pm c_s\). The same invariant-speed demand therefore produces a sound-based relativity of simultaneity and a formal limiting speed \(c_s\).

Deep Notes

This section is a complete derivation and interpretation. It begins from the invariant-signal requirement, derives the Lorentz form without relying on the other tabs, and then repeats the construction explicitly for sound.

1. What is and is not being explained

A moving passenger who receives one front before another establishes only unequal arrival events along that passenger's path. No transformation of space or time is needed to explain that fact. The substantive construction begins when two inertial coordinate systems are both required to assign the same propagation speed to the chosen signal.

Let that required invariant speed be \(w\). The derivation does not initially decide whether \(w\) is the speed of light, sound, or another messenger.

2. General linear transformation

Let the origins of \(S\) and \(S'\) coincide at \(t=t'=0\), and let \(S'\) move at constant speed \(v\) along the common x-axis. Homogeneity and uniform motion motivate a linear transformation. Write

\[x'=a(x-vt).\]

This guarantees that the origin of \(S'\), whose path in \(S\) is \(x=vt\), always has \(x'=0\). Allow the transformed time to depend on both time and position:

\[t'=b(t-\kappa x).\]

The unknown \(\kappa\) is essential. If \(\kappa=0\), time remains universal and the transformation reduces to a scaled Galilean form.

3. Right-moving invariant front

A right-moving signal front in \(S\) satisfies

\[x=wt.\]

Substitution into the transformation gives

\[x'=a(w-v)t,\qquad t'=b(1-\kappa w)t.\]

Requiring the same speed in \(S'\), \(x'/t'=w\), gives

\[a(w-v)=wb(1-\kappa w).\tag{1}\]

4. Left-moving invariant front

A left-moving front satisfies \(x=-wt\). Substitution gives

\[x'=-a(w+v)t,\qquad t'=b(1+\kappa w)t.\]

Requiring \(x'/t'=-w\) gives

\[a(w+v)=wb(1+\kappa w).\tag{2}\]

5. Determining the position dependence of time

Add equations (1) and (2):

\[2aw=2wb,\]

so \(a=b\). Subtract (1) from (2):

\[2av=2wb\kappa w.\]

Using \(a=b\),

\[\kappa=\frac{v}{w^2}.\]

The transformed time must therefore contain

\[-\frac{vx}{w^2}.\]

This term is not added after relativity of simultaneity is discovered. It is the term required to keep both signal directions at the same slope \(\pm w\). Because it depends on position, events at different locations that share one value of \(t\) generally receive different values of \(t'\).

6. Determining the scale factor

The transformation now has the form

\[x'=a(x-vt),\qquad t'=a\left(t-\frac{vx}{w^2}\right).\]

Demanding reciprocity—that the inverse transformation have the same form with \(v\to -v\)—gives

\[a^2\left(1-\frac{v^2}{w^2}\right)=1.\]

Choosing the positive factor that approaches one as \(v\to0\),

\[a=\gamma_w=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/w^2}}.\]

Hence

\[\boxed{x'=\gamma_w(x-vt)},\] \[\boxed{t'=\gamma_w\left(t-\frac{vx}{w^2}\right)}.\]

7. Direct verification

For the right-moving front \(x=wt\),

\[x'=\gamma_w(w-v)t,\] \[t'=\gamma_w\left(1-\frac{v}{w}\right)t.\]

Since \(w-v=w(1-v/w)\),

\[\frac{x'}{t'}=w.\]

The left-moving front similarly gives \(-w\). The transformation was designed precisely to preserve these two signal slopes.

8. Light case

Setting \(w=c\) gives the ordinary Lorentz transformation:

\[x'=\gamma(x-vt),\qquad t'=\gamma\left(t-\frac{vx}{c^2}\right),\qquad \gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}.\]

The mathematical structure follows from linearity, reciprocity, and the demand that light retain speed \(c\) in both directions in every inertial coordinate system.

9. Sound case from measured wave quantities

Consider sound propagating through a medium at speed \(c_s\). For a moving receiver, let the measured frequency and physically perceived wavelength transform together so that

\[f'_s\lambda'_s=c_s.\]

The values \(c_s-v\) and \(c_s+v\) describe arrival rates at selected fronts; they are not taken as the propagation speed reconstructed from the receiver's frequency and perceived wavelength.

If every inertial coordinate system is required to preserve the measured propagation value \(c_s\), substitute \(w=c_s\) into the general result:

\[x'_s=\gamma_s(x-vt),\] \[t'_s=\gamma_s\left(t-\frac{vx}{c_s^2}\right),\] \[\gamma_s=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c_s^2}}.\]

A sound front satisfying \(x=c_st\) becomes

\[x'_s=\gamma_s(c_s-v)t,\] \[t'_s=\gamma_s\left(1-\frac{v}{c_s}\right)t,\]

and therefore

\[\frac{x'_s}{t'_s}=c_s.\]

The opposite direction gives \(-c_s\). The sonic transformation therefore produces the same formal features as the light transformation: position-dependent distant time, relativity of simultaneity, and an invariant messenger speed.

10. The apparent limiting speed

As \(v\) approaches the selected invariant value,

\[\gamma_w=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/w^2}}\longrightarrow\infty.\]

For the sonic construction this divergence occurs at \(v=c_s\). Yet matter can move faster than sound. The formal limit therefore belongs at least to the coordinate family constructed under the invariant-sound demand; it cannot by itself establish a universal limit on every possible physical process.

11. What the train argument actually establishes

The train argument becomes significant not because a traveller changes the order in which information arrives, but because the internal description is required to preserve the messenger speed through a position-dependent transformation of time. The mathematics is consistent. The interpretive question is what has been established about the world beyond that signal-based coordinate construction.

The sound substitution supplies the control case: the same invariant-speed logic can generate the same Lorentz form and an apparent limit at a speed already known not to bound all motion. This does not by itself disprove the light-based transformation. It shows that the existence of the form is not sufficient to prove that its invariant messenger speed is necessarily the universal boundary of nature.

Connection to the novel

Jan stops treating the train as a puzzle about which flash reaches one passenger first. The useful question is why the internal and external descriptions of the messenger must be made equivalent by transforming distant time.

Sound then gives him the control case: the same demand can build the same formal geometry around a different wave speed.
