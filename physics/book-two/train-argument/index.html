What the Train Argument Actually Establishes

A passenger meeting one flash before another is ordinary message-arrival geometry. Relativity of simultaneity enters only when a stronger rule is imposed on distant coordinates.

Published essay

Three layers to keep separate

Reception order is not yet a transformation of time.

Keep distinct the propagation of the wave, the arrival of its message at a moving receiver, and the coordinate rule used to compare distant events in different inertial systems.

Intuition

Why the passenger receives one flash first

Imagine two flashes produced at opposite ends of a railway embankment while a passenger sits at the midpoint of a moving train. In the embankment description, both light fronts propagate at \(c\). The passenger moves towards one travelling message and away from the other.

So the two remaining separations do not close at the same rate:

\[c+v\qquad\text{and}\qquad c-v.\]

The passenger therefore need not receive the two messages together even if the source events were simultaneous in the embankment coordinates.

Unequal reception establishes unequal meetings with travelling messages. By itself it does not establish that the source events occurred at different times.

The stronger step

Special relativity goes further. The train is also given its own spatial grid and its own synchronized clocks. Those clocks are coordinated so that light has the same one-way coordinate speed \(c\) in both directions.

Once that condition is imposed, the train cannot keep the embankment’s distant simultaneity. Its time coordinate must depend on position.

The issue examined here is therefore not the simple fact that a moving passenger receives one flash first. It is the stronger identification:

\[\text{wave speed }c\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{one-way message speed }c\text{ in every inertial coordinate system}.\]

The Argument

1. Reception geometry requires no relativity of simultaneity

In one chosen frame, let two light messages move towards a receiver. The receiver’s motion changes where its worldline intersects each light front. The encounter rates can be \(c+v\) and \(c-v\) while each light front itself still propagates at \(c\) in that same frame.

That statement alone is ordinary kinematics.

2. Now impose an invariant coordinate signal speed

Let two inertial systems \(S\) and \(S'\) move at relative speed \(v\). Suppose a wave has propagation speed \(w\) in \(S\), with fronts

\[x=wt,\qquad x=-wt.\]

Now impose the stronger requirement that these same fronts must also satisfy

\[x'=wt',\qquad x'=-wt'\]

in \(S'\). This turns \(w\) into the same one-way coordinate speed in both inertial descriptions.

3. A position-dependent time coordinate is then required

Assume a linear transformation

\[x'=a(x-vt),\qquad t'=b(t-\kappa x).\]

Applying it to the right-moving front gives

\[a(w-v)=wb(1-\kappa w).\]

Applying it to the left-moving front gives

\[a(w+v)=wb(1+\kappa w).\]

Adding and subtracting yield

\[a=b,\qquad \kappa=\frac{v}{w^2}.\]

Thus the time coordinate must contain

\[-\frac{vx}{w^2}.\]

That term changes the times assigned to distant events.

4. Reciprocity fixes the Lorentz-form scale

If the inverse transformation is required to have the same form with \(v\to -v\), then

\[a=\gamma_w=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/w^2}}.\]

The transformation becomes

\[\boxed{x'=\gamma_w(x-vt)},\]
\[\boxed{t'=\gamma_w\left(t-\frac{vx}{w^2}\right)}.\]

The Lorentz form has appeared because the same two-way wave speed was promoted to the same one-way coordinate speed in both systems, together with linearity and reciprocity.

5. For light

Set \(w=c\):

\[x'=\gamma(x-vt),\qquad t'=\gamma\left(t-\frac{vx}{c^2}\right).\]

This is the ordinary Lorentz transformation.

6. Why sound is a useful control example

Sound gives a system where the distinction between wave propagation and message arrival is easy to see. Relative to the medium, the sound wave propagates at \(c_s\). A moving receiver can reconstruct the same local wave relation while a message reaches that moving endpoint at \(c_s\pm v\).

If we nevertheless impose the stronger coordinate rule

\[x'_s/t'_s=\pm c_s\]

for every inertial system, the identical derivation produces

\[x'_s=\gamma_s(x-vt),\qquad t'_s=\gamma_s\left(t-\frac{vx}{c_s^2}\right),\]
\[\gamma_s=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c_s^2}}.\]

That does not make nature sonic-relativistic. It shows what the invariant-coordinate-speed requirement mathematically does.

7. The question left open

The train reception argument establishes where moving receivers meet travelling signals. The Lorentz transformation establishes a coordinate structure under stronger assumptions. Treating the first as though it alone proved the second skips the distinction this path is designed to expose.

Deep Notes

This section starts from the beginning. It first derives the train reception geometry without any transformation of time. It then separately derives the Lorentz form from an invariant one-way coordinate-speed requirement and uses sound as a control case.

1. The physical train problem

Take an embankment frame \(S\). Two source events occur at separated locations. Their light signals propagate through \(S\) at speed \(c\). A passenger moves at speed \(v\).

The reception events are found by intersecting the passenger worldline with the two light-front worldlines. If the passenger moves towards one incoming front, their separation can close at

\[c+v.\]

If the passenger moves away from the other, the separation can close at

\[c-v.\]

These are message-arrival or encounter rates in the selected frame. They are not new wave propagation speeds.

2. What unequal reception proves

If the passenger receives the first message before the second, then the two reception events on the passenger’s worldline are different.

That observation alone does not determine whether the two source events were simultaneous, because simultaneity concerns the timing assigned to spatially separated source events, not merely the later order in which their messages arrive.

To infer distant source timing from reception, one must specify how propagation delay and receiver motion are being reconstructed.

3. A separate mathematical problem

Now forget the particular train for a moment. Ask instead what coordinate transformation is required if a chosen signal speed \(w\) is to have the same value in both directions in every inertial coordinate system.

Let

\[x=wt\]

and

\[x=-wt\]

be the two signal fronts in \(S\). Let \(S'\) move at \(v\), and assume

\[x'=a(x-vt),\qquad t'=b(t-\kappa x).\]

The position dependence in \(t'\) is left open rather than assumed away.

4. Preserve the right-moving coordinate speed

For \(x=wt\),

\[x'=a(w-v)t,\]
\[t'=b(1-\kappa w)t.\]

Demanding \(x'/t'=w\) gives

\[a(w-v)=wb(1-\kappa w).\tag{1}\]

5. Preserve the left-moving coordinate speed

For \(x=-wt\),

\[x'=-a(w+v)t,\]
\[t'=b(1+\kappa w)t.\]

Demanding \(x'/t'=-w\) gives

\[a(w+v)=wb(1+\kappa w).\tag{2}\]

6. The invariant-speed requirement fixes distant time

Add equations (1) and (2):

\[2aw=2wb,\]

so

\[a=b.\]

Subtracting them gives

\[2av=2wb\kappa w,\]

hence

\[\boxed{\kappa=\frac{v}{w^2}}.\]

Therefore

\[t'=a\left(t-\frac{vx}{w^2}\right).\]

Relativity of distant simultaneity is already present in the position-dependent term. It is the mathematical price of preserving the same one-way coordinate signal speed in both directions.

7. Reciprocity fixes the remaining factor

Require the inverse transformation to have the same functional form when \(v\) changes sign. Then

\[a^2\left(1-\frac{v^2}{w^2}\right)=1,\]

so

\[a=\gamma_w=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/w^2}}.\]

The resulting transformation is

\[\boxed{x'=\gamma_w(x-vt)},\]
\[\boxed{t'=\gamma_w\left(t-\frac{vx}{w^2}\right)}.\]

8. What this derivation actually demonstrates

The algebra does not discover which physical signal nature must make invariant. It answers a conditional question:

If a speed \(w\) is required to be the same one-way coordinate speed in both directions in every inertial system, what linear reciprocal transformation preserves it?

The answer is a Lorentz-form transformation with invariant parameter \(w\).

This is mathematically important, but it keeps the assumption visible.

9. The light substitution

For special relativity, set

\[w=c.\]

Then

\[x'=\gamma(x-vt),\qquad t'=\gamma\left(t-\frac{vx}{c^2}\right).\]

In standard SR, this is supported not only by the train story but by the relativity principle, Maxwellian electrodynamics and a large experimental structure. The narrow point here is that the train’s unequal reception by itself is not the derivation.

10. The sound control experiment in thought

Sound propagates through a material medium at \(c_s\). A receiver moving relative to that medium may still reconstruct a local wave relation

\[f_R\lambda_R=c_s,\]

while a specific message sent across an initial separation to that moving receiver has an encounter rate

\[c_s-v\]

or

\[c_s+v.\]

Now deliberately make the stronger coordinate identification:

\[\text{wave speed }c_s=\text{one-way message coordinate speed in every inertial frame}.\]

Substituting \(w=c_s\) produces

\[x'_s=\gamma_s(x-vt),\]
\[t'_s=\gamma_s\left(t-\frac{vx}{c_s^2}\right),\]
\[\gamma_s=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c_s^2}}.\]

The formal structure now has a limiting value at \(c_s\), even though objects can physically move faster than sound.

That does not refute Lorentz invariance for light. It demonstrates that the existence of a Lorentz-form transformation follows from the invariant-speed postulate and reciprocity; the physical status of the selected invariant speed requires separate evidence.

11. Why wave speed and message speed must stay separate

This is the central terminology of the revised Path II.

A wave speed follows the propagating disturbance:

\[v_{wave}=\frac{d x_{front}}{dt}.\]

A message speed to a specified moving endpoint refers to completion of an emission-to-reception task:

\[v_{msg}=\frac{\text{initial source–receiver separation}}{\text{emission-to-reception time}}.\]

For a receding receiver in one external frame, these can be

\[v_{wave}=c,\qquad v_{msg}=c-v.\]

The fact that a moving coordinate system later assigns the wave the value \(c\) again does not erase the need to state which quotient is being discussed.

12. Open obligation

The portal’s alternative reading remains incomplete until it identifies an experiment that distinguishes two physical claims:

\[\text{local/reconstructed wave speed remains }c\]

versus

\[\text{one-way message transfer to every moving receiver is physically }c.\]

The test must define clocks and distances without using the disputed one-way synchronization rule to build the answer in advance.

Until then, the train argument establishes an important separation: reception geometry is one problem; the transformation of distant time is another.

Connection to the novel

Jan’s train question is not “which flash did the passenger see first?” That part is straightforward.

The deeper question is why a later coordinate reconstruction of distant time should be allowed to redefine the physical speed of the message that produced the reception events.
