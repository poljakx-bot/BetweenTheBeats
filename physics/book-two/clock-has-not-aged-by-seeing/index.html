The Clock Has Not Aged by Seeing

A receiver can change the cadence at which it receives the marks of a distant clock. That change does not, by itself, establish that the clock generated those marks at a different physical rate.

Published essay

Four questions to keep separate

Measurement is not the same thing as its explanation.

As you read, keep four tasks distinct: the standard account; what the measurement directly establishes; the portal’s objection or alternative; and the evidence that could distinguish the two.

Intuition

A clock does not send its time directly.

It produces physical marks in an outgoing electromagnetic signal:

\[S_1,S_2,S_3,\ldots,S_N.\]

Once emitted, the marks travel away from the clock.

Now suppose the clock finishes producing the entire sequence. Only afterward, the receiver changes direction.

By moving towards the travelling sequence, the receiver encounters the remaining marks more quickly. By moving away, it encounters them more slowly.

The reception cadence changes.

But the clock has already completed the history that produced the marks. The receiver cannot change their number, order, source timestamps or generation intervals.

Therefore,

\[\boxed{ \text{received cadence} \neq \text{source clock history}. }\]

A changed report about a clock is not automatically a changed clock.

The Argument

Let a clock at (x=0) emit (N) distinguishable marks at

\[t_n=nT, \qquad n=0,1,\ldots,N-1.\]

Each mark propagates in the positive (x) direction at (c):

\[x_n(t)=c(t-t_n).\]

Their order and source-coordinate emission interval are fixed:

\[S_1,S_2,\ldots,S_N,\]
\[t_{n+1}-t_n=T.\]

Approaching the sequence

Let a receiver approach from position (D) with coordinate trajectory

\[x_R(t)=D-vt.\]

It encounters mark (S_n) when

\[D-vt_{R,n}=c(t_{R,n}-t_n).\]

Thus,

\[t_{R,n}=\frac{D+ct_n}{c+v},\]

and successive reception events are separated in this source-frame coordinate description by

\[\Delta t_R = \frac{cT}{c+v} = \frac{T}{1+v/c}.\]

Receding from the sequence

For a receiver moving away,

\[x_R(t)=D+vt,\]

so

\[\Delta t_R = \frac{T}{1-v/c}.\]

The same finite record can therefore be encountered at different coordinate cadences depending on the receiver trajectory.

These expressions describe encounter timing in the chosen source coordinates. A receiver’s locally measured proper-time cadence requires the additional relation between those coordinates and the receiver’s clock.

Change motion after the final emission

Let the final mark be emitted at (t_{N-1}), and let the receiver change direction only at

\[t_{\mathrm{turn}}>t_{N-1}.\]

By then, every mark already exists in the travelling field. The clock may have stopped transmitting or even been removed.

The receiver’s later motion can change:

  • the arrival time of each remaining mark;
  • the interval between future arrivals;
  • the received frequency;
  • the location of each encounter.

It cannot change:

  • the number of marks;
  • their order;
  • their source timestamps;
  • the completed generation history.

The receiver changes the mapping

\[t_{\mathrm{emit}} \longrightarrow t_{\mathrm{receive}},\]

not the already completed source sequence.

Encoded timestamps

Suppose successive marks explicitly carry the source readings

\[10.0\ \mathrm{s}, \quad 11.0\ \mathrm{s}, \quad 12.0\ \mathrm{s}.\]

An approaching receiver may encounter them at local readings

\[20.0\ \mathrm{s}, \quad 20.8\ \mathrm{s}, \quad 21.6\ \mathrm{s}.\]

The arrival interval is (0.8) seconds, but the encoded source readings remain one second apart.

The signal itself contains two different temporal structures:

\[\text{interval between generation events}\]

and

\[\text{interval between reception events}.\]

A theory may relate them. It must not silently identify them.

Seeing a clock and comparing clocks

Remote observation follows the chain

\[\text{clock} \rightarrow \text{signal} \rightarrow \text{propagation} \rightarrow \text{receiver}.\]

A local clock comparison occurs when two clocks are brought together and their displayed readings are compared at one place.

These are different physical procedures.

A changed received cadence can be explained through propagation and encounter. A difference between reunited clocks requires an account of the physical histories of the clocks themselves.

Deep Notes

A finite record already in flight

A live transmission can hide the distinction between generation and reception because the source is still operating while the receiver observes it.

The finite-sequence experiment removes that ambiguity.

All marks are emitted first. The source then stops. Only afterward does the receiver alter its motion.

There is no continuing source process whose rate could be changed. There is only a completed source history, a spatially distributed travelling record and a receiver following a new trajectory through it.

Temporal structure becomes spatial structure

After emission, the source interval (T) appears as a spatial separation between marks in the source-frame description:

\[\Delta x=cT.\]

The receiver moves through this existing spatial sequence.

Approach shortens the interval between encounters:

\[\Delta t_{\mathrm{enc}}=\frac{\Delta x}{c+v}.\]

Recession lengthens it:

\[\Delta t_{\mathrm{enc}}=\frac{\Delta x}{c-v}.\]

The receiver changes how rapidly it crosses the record. It does not change the spacing already produced in that description.

Retarded time and clock readings

At reception time (t_R), the arriving field represents the source at an earlier retarded time (t_{\mathrm{ret}}).

For a static source,

\[t_R-t_{\mathrm{ret}}=\frac{x_R(t_R)}{c}.\]

When the receiver changes trajectory, the mapping between successive values of (t_R) and (t_{\mathrm{ret}}) changes.

The receiver can therefore advance through the recorded source history at a different cadence without changing how that history was generated.

Doppler shift describes a receiver-signal relation

A moving receiver measures a changed frequency. The Doppler shift is real.

It directly establishes a changed relation between:

  • the travelling wave structure;
  • the receiver trajectory;
  • the receiver’s local clock.

To conclude from the received frequency alone that the distant source clock accumulated a different proper time requires an additional theoretical connection.

In standard special relativity, the longitudinal Doppler factor for approach is

\[\frac{f_R}{f_S} = \sqrt{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}}, \qquad \beta=\frac{v}{c},\]

with the reciprocal factor for recession.

Book Two does not deny that measured relation. It separates the observation from the claim about the completed source history.

Acceleration clarifies what changes

Suppose marks (S_1) through (S_5) have already been received, while (S_6) through (S_{10}) remain in flight. The receiver then turns.

The earlier emission events remain fixed. The earlier reception events remain fixed. Only the later intersections between the receiver trajectory and the remaining marks change.

The distant source need perform no corresponding action when the receiver turns. In the finite-sequence version, it is no longer transmitting at all.

Mutual seeing is not reunion

During separation, two clocks exchange delayed and Doppler-shifted reports.

The final empirical statement of a twin or transported-clock experiment is different: the clocks are brought together and their displayed readings are compared locally.

A complete explanation of that result must address the clocks’ trajectories and operation. It cannot be reduced to what each clock remotely appeared to do.

Proper time remains an obligation

Standard special relativity assigns proper time along a trajectory by

\[\Delta\tau = \int \sqrt{1-\frac{v^2(t)}{c^2}}\,dt\]

in flat spacetime relative to an inertial coordinate description.

This predicts different accumulated readings for clocks following different paths.

The finite-signal argument does not by itself refute those predictions. It establishes that a Doppler-shifted report is not identical to the physical aging of the clock being reported.

Any alternative account must still explain:

  • reunited atomic clocks;
  • transported-clock experiments;
  • unstable-particle lifetimes;
  • trajectory-dependent accumulated readings.

That burden should be addressed as a problem of clocks and their physical histories, not hidden inside a discussion of travelling reports.

Several receivers, one completed source history

Imagine three receivers following different trajectories through the same finite signal.

One approaches, one remains stationary and one recedes. They encounter the marks at three different cadences.

The completed source cannot have generated the same sequence at three different physical rates.

The differing cadences belong to the three receiver-signal relations. The common source history remains encoded in the order and content of the marks.

What this theme establishes

The receiver can alter future reception cadence after the source history is complete.

Therefore,

\[\boxed{ \text{received cadence is not, by itself, the physical rate of the distant clock.} }\]

This does not prove that all clocks accumulate equal proper time. It establishes that clock history, signal history and reception history must be kept distinct.

Further reading

  • Albert Einstein, “On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies” (1905)
  • Wolfgang Rindler, Introduction to Special Relativity

Connection to the novel

Jan’s decisive thought experiment begins after the clock has finished speaking.

The marks are already in flight. Then the receiver turns, and the reports begin arriving at a new rhythm.

The changed rhythm is real. The receiver’s clock records it. But the distant clock did not return to its completed past and regenerate the marks.

The observer changed its relation to the messenger.
