Einstein Synchronization: What Is Measured and What Is Assigned

Einstein synchronization constructs a shared distant time by assigning the reflection event to the midpoint of one clock’s round-trip readings.

Published essay

Intuition

A clock at A can directly record when a signal leaves and when it returns. It cannot directly read the time shown by a different clock at B.

Einstein synchronization supplies the missing remote time by placing the reflection event halfway between the departure and return readings:

\[t_B=\frac{t_A+t'_A}{2}.\]

This is an extraordinarily useful coordination rule. The question is what has been measured and what has been assigned.

When the laboratory moves relative to an external observer, the outward and return journeys have different arrival rates. Treating them as equal one-way journeys inside the laboratory therefore requires a different assignment of time at separated locations.

The Argument

Einstein’s 1905 construction

A signal leaves clock A when it reads (t_A), reaches and is reflected at B when the B clock reads (t_B), and returns to A when the A clock reads (t'_A).

Einstein defines the clocks as synchronous when

\[t_B-t_A=t'_A-t_B.\]

Solving for the remote B reading gives

\[t_B=\frac{t_A+t'_A}{2}.\]

The departure and return readings are recorded by one clock at A. The placement of the B event at the midpoint is the synchronization rule that creates a shared time across the distance AB.

A moving laboratory seen from outside

Let A and B be separated initially by (L) in an external system, and let the laboratory move in the A-to-B direction at speed (v).

On the outward journey the signal must catch B. The remaining distance closes at (c-v), so

\[t_{AB}=\frac{L}{c-v}.\]

After reflection, the signal and A move towards one another. Their separation closes at (c+v), so

\[t_{BA}=\frac{L}{c+v}.\]

The total external time is

\[T=\frac{L}{c-v}+\frac{L}{c+v}=\frac{2Lc}{c^2-v^2}.\]

The effective round-trip rate across two laboratory separations

If the reference distance is taken as two copies of the original separation, (2L), the effective round-trip traversal rate is

\[\bar c_{\rm lab}=\frac{2L}{T}=\frac{c^2-v^2}{c}=c\left(1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right).\]

For every non-zero (v),

\[\boxed{\bar c_{\rm lab}

The slower outward leg dominates the harmonic mean. The faster return leg cannot cancel it because equal distances are not averaged by simply averaging the two speeds.

The light front itself still travels at (c)

The actual external path lengths are

\[d_{AB}=ct_{AB}=\frac{cL}{c-v},\qquad d_{BA}=ct_{BA}=\frac{cL}{c+v}.\]

Dividing each actual path by its time gives (c). The smaller quantity above is therefore not a reduced propagation speed of the front. It is the effective rate for completing two moving-endpoint separations.

Deep Notes

This section independently reconstructs Einstein synchronization and the moving-endpoint round trip. It defines every time, derives the unequal external legs, and explains exactly which quantity is measured and which is assigned.

1. Local readings and a distant event

Place clock A at one end of a laboratory and clock B at the other. A signal leaves A when the local clock reads \(t_A\), is reflected at B when the B clock reads \(t_B\), and returns to A when the same A clock reads \(t'_A\).

Clock A directly records only two local events: emission and return. The round-trip interval

\[t'_A-t_A\]

is therefore a one-clock measurement. The reflection event occurs at B, so its time cannot be read directly from clock A.

2. Einstein's synchronization rule

Einstein defines the two clocks as synchronized when the outward and return one-way intervals are equal:

\[t_B-t_A=t'_A-t_B.\]

Solving this condition gives

\[\boxed{t_B=\frac{t_A+t'_A}{2}}.\]

The midpoint value is not another reading made by clock A. It is the remote-time assignment that creates a shared coordinate time across A and B. Once adopted, the convention makes the one-way travel times equal by construction and permits the one-way signal speed to be stated as equal in both directions.

3. The same round trip viewed from an external system

Now describe a laboratory moving from A towards B at speed \(v\) relative to an external coordinate system. At emission, let the external separation of the endpoints be \(L\). A signal propagates through that system at speed \(c\).

On the outward leg, endpoint B moves away while the front follows it. The separation closes at \(c-v\), so

\[(c-v)t_{AB}=L,\qquad t_{AB}=\frac{L}{c-v}.\]

After reflection, the signal and endpoint A move towards one another. Their separation closes at \(c+v\), so

\[(c+v)t_{BA}=L,\qquad t_{BA}=\frac{L}{c+v}.\]

The external system therefore describes unequal one-way encounter times even though the internal synchronization rule assigns equal one-way coordinate times.

4. Total round-trip time

Adding the two external intervals gives

\[T=\frac{L}{c-v}+\frac{L}{c+v}.\]

Using a common denominator,

\[T=\frac{L(c+v)+L(c-v)}{c^2-v^2}=\frac{2Lc}{c^2-v^2}.\]

For any non-zero \(v\), the denominator is smaller than \(c^2\), and therefore

\[T>\frac{2L}{c}.\]

The slower outward leg adds more time than the faster return leg removes.

5. Effective traversal rate across two laboratory separations

If the reference distance is taken as two copies of the initial endpoint separation, \(2L\), the effective round-trip traversal rate is

\[\bar c_{\rm lab}=\frac{2L}{T}=\frac{2L}{2Lc/(c^2-v^2)}=\frac{c^2-v^2}{c}.\]

Thus

\[\boxed{\bar c_{\rm lab}=c\left(1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)This is the harmonic mean of the two arrival rates: \[\bar c_{\rm lab}=\frac{2}{\frac{1}{c-v}+\frac{1}{c+v}}.\]

It is not obtained by the arithmetic average \(((c-v)+(c+v))/2=c\), because equal distances travelled at different rates must be weighted by the times required to traverse them.

6. What depends on distance

The fractional reduction of the effective rate is

\[\frac{c-\bar c_{\rm lab}}{c}=\frac{v^2}{c^2}.\]

It depends only on the speed ratio \(v/c\), not on the laboratory separation. The absolute additional round-trip time is

\[\Delta T=T-\frac{2L}{c}=\frac{2Lv^2}{c(c^2-v^2)}.\]

This delay grows linearly with \(L\). Distance therefore controls how much timing difference accumulates, not the fractional reduction of the effective rate.

7. The light front still propagates at \(c\)

The actual external path lengths are not both \(L\). During the outward journey B moves forward, so the light covers

\[d_{AB}=ct_{AB}=\frac{cL}{c-v}.\]

During the return journey A approaches, so the light covers

\[d_{BA}=ct_{BA}=\frac{cL}{c+v}.\]

Dividing each actual path by its external time gives

\[\frac{d_{AB}}{t_{AB}}=c,\qquad \frac{d_{BA}}{t_{BA}}=c.\]

The smaller effective traversal rate therefore does not claim that the front itself propagated below \(c\). It describes how rapidly a round trip is completed when the reference distance is the moving laboratory's endpoint separation.

8. Measurement versus assignment

The external unequal legs are obtained from one coordinate system following moving endpoints. The internal equality of one-way times is obtained from a network of distant clocks coordinated by the midpoint rule. Both descriptions can be mathematically complete, but they do not arise from the same set of direct readings.

The precise distinction established here is:

  • the round-trip interval at A is directly measured by one clock;
  • the time assigned to the reflection at B depends on the synchronization rule;
  • equal internal one-way times are a property of the resulting coordinate grid;
  • the external observer retains unequal arrival times and actual front speed \(c\).

This section stops at synchronization. It does not derive the transformation between inertial systems; that is a separate question.

Connection to the novel

Jan returns to the 1905 paper not to dispute the usefulness of synchronization, but to locate the exact point at which a practical rule for distant clocks becomes a statement about distant time itself.

The rule works. His question is what physical claim has been added to its success.
