Einstein Synchronization: What Is Measured and What Is Assigned

A round trip can be measured by one clock. A one-way message time between separated clocks requires a rule for distant time. Einstein’s rule makes that distinction explicit.

Published essay

Three quantities to keep separate

Wave propagation, message arrival and distant clock time are not the same object.

The wave can propagate at \(c\) in a chosen frame, a moving endpoint can meet the message at a rate \(c\pm v\), and a network of distant clocks can be synchronized so that its assigned one-way coordinate speed is \(c\). This page asks where each statement comes from.

Intuition

One clock can measure a round trip

Place clock A at one end of a laboratory and clock B at the other. A sends a light message to B, B reflects it, and the message returns to A.

Clock A can directly record two local events: emission and return. It therefore measures the complete round-trip duration without needing any distant clock at all.

\[T_{round}=t'_A-t_A.\]

The one-way time is a different problem

To say how long the message took from A to B, we need a time reading at B that is comparable with the reading at A. Einstein’s 1905 construction supplies that common distant time by defining the clocks as synchronous when

\[t_B-t_A=t'_A-t_B.\]

Equivalently,

\[\boxed{t_B=\frac{t_A+t'_A}{2}}.\]

The midpoint is not a third reading made by clock A. It is the rule used to coordinate the remote clock.

The round trip is measured locally. The split into two equal one-way times is part of the synchronization procedure.

Why a moving receiver matters

Now describe the same kind of message in an external frame while the receiving endpoint moves. The light front may still propagate at \(c\), but the remaining separation between message and receiver closes at \(c-v\) or \(c+v\). That changes the arrival event without changing the chosen-frame propagation speed of the wave.

The central question is therefore not whether Einstein synchronization is useful. It is whether a coordinate rule that makes the one-way speed equal to \(c\) should also be read as an independent measurement that the physical message reached every moving receiver at \(c\).

The Argument

1. Einstein first identifies the distant-time problem

A local clock is sufficient for events occurring beside it. Events at A and B cannot be placed on one common time scale until an additional relation between the two clocks is supplied.

Einstein supplies that relation with a light signal. If the message leaves A at \(t_A\), is reflected at B when B reads \(t_B\), and returns to A at \(t'_A\), the clocks are defined as synchronous when

\[t_B-t_A=t'_A-t_B.\]

So

\[t_B=\frac{t_A+t'_A}{2}.\]

2. What is directly measured

The two A-events are local to one clock. Their difference

\[t'_A-t_A\]

is directly measurable as a round-trip interval. The division of that interval into equal outward and return parts is the condition used to synchronize the separated clocks.

3. Separate wave speed from message speed

Take one external coordinate system. At the instant of emission let A and B be separated by \(L\), and let both endpoints move to the right at speed \(v\). Let the light front propagate to the right at \(c\) in this chosen frame.

For the outward message, B recedes while the front approaches it:

\[(c-v)t_{AB}=L,\qquad t_{AB}=\frac{L}{c-v}.\]

If we define the message-transfer rate across that initial endpoint separation by

\[v_{msg,AB}\equiv\frac{L}{t_{AB}},\]

then

\[\boxed{v_{msg,AB}=c-v}.\]

The wave front itself has not slowed. Its chosen-frame path remains \(x_F=ct\). The smaller quantity describes the rate at which the message closes the original source-to-receiver separation.

4. The return message is the opposite geometry

After reflection, A moves towards the returning front. The endpoint separation is again \(L\), so

\[(c+v)t_{BA}=L,\qquad t_{BA}=\frac{L}{c+v}.\]

Thus

\[\boxed{v_{msg,BA}=c+v}.\]

The two message rates are unequal even though the wave propagation rate in the selected external frame is \(c\) on both legs.

5. The round-trip message rate

The total external coordinate time is

\[T=\frac{L}{c-v}+\frac{L}{c+v}=\frac{2Lc}{c^2-v^2}.\]

If we ask how rapidly the two endpoint separations \(L+L\) are completed by the outward and return messages, the effective rate is

\[v_{msg,round}=\frac{2L}{T}=c\left(1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right).\]

This is not the physical path speed of the light front. The actual path lengths are \(ct_{AB}\) and \(ct_{BA}\), and each divided by its external time is still \(c\).

6. Where synchronization enters

Inside the moving laboratory, Einstein synchronization defines a new distant time coordinate by applying the same midpoint condition to clocks at rest in that laboratory. With that coordinate assignment, the one-way coordinate travel times are equal and the one-way coordinate speed is \(c\).

The portal’s distinction is therefore:

\[\text{wave propagation}\neq\text{message-to-moving-endpoint rate}\neq\text{remote-time assignment}.\]

7. The precise question

Standard special relativity treats the synchronization rule, the relativity principle and the Lorentz transformation as one consistent spacetime construction. The portal does not need to deny that mathematical consistency.

Its narrower question is operational: when the one-way speed of a message is stated as \(c\), how much of that statement came from a direct one-way measurement and how much came from the clock network used to define distant simultaneity?

Deep Notes

This section starts from the beginning. It reconstructs Einstein’s 1905 synchronization procedure, separates the wave from the message, derives the moving-endpoint arrival times, and then shows where the moving-frame time coordinate enters.

1. Why distant time needs a procedure

A clock beside an event can assign that event a local reading. If a train reaches A while clock A reads 7, the local statement requires no remote synchronization.

But suppose event \(E_A\) occurs beside clock A and event \(E_B\) occurs beside clock B. Before comparing their times, the readings of A and B must belong to a common timing convention. Einstein explicitly begins his 1905 construction from this problem: local A-time and local B-time do not yet constitute one common time for separated places.

2. Einstein’s light-signal rule

Let a light signal leave A at A-time \(t_A\), reach B at B-time \(t_B\), and return to A at A-time \(t'_A\). Einstein defines the clocks as synchronous when the two one-way intervals are equal:

\[t_B-t_A=t'_A-t_B.\]

Solving gives

\[\boxed{t_B=\frac{t_A+t'_A}{2}}.\]

This equation does two jobs at once. It gives a practical rule for setting distant clocks, and it creates the time coordinate with which one-way travel between A and B will subsequently be described.

3. What one clock establishes without synchronization

Clock A directly measures

\[T_A=t'_A-t_A.\]

If the A–B distance is known, this supports a two-way or round-trip signal-speed measurement. It does not, by itself, tell us how much of \(T_A\) belonged to A→B and how much to B→A.

The equality

\[t_B-t_A=t'_A-t_B\]

supplies that split. It is therefore essential to distinguish

\[\text{measured round trip}\quad\text{from}\quad\text{assigned one-way times}.\]

4. Einstein himself obtains unequal moving-endpoint legs in the stationary description

Now take a rod whose endpoints move at speed \(v\) through Einstein’s stationary coordinate system. Let its endpoint separation, measured in that stationary system, be \(L\). In the 1905 paper the outward and return intervals are written in the form

\[t_{AB}=\frac{L}{c-v},\qquad t_{BA}=\frac{L}{c+v}.\]

The reason is purely geometric. On the outward leg the receiver B recedes from the front. On the return leg A approaches it.

These are message-to-endpoint encounter times in one coordinate system. They do not assert that the electromagnetic wave itself propagated at \(c-v\) or \(c+v\).

5. Wave path and message completion are different quotients

For the outward leg, the actual chosen-frame path of the light front is

\[d_{wave,AB}=ct_{AB}=\frac{cL}{c-v}.\]

Therefore

\[\frac{d_{wave,AB}}{t_{AB}}=c.\]

But if the question is how quickly the original endpoint separation \(L\) was closed, then

\[\frac{L}{t_{AB}}=c-v.\]

Both equations are true because their numerators describe different distances.

The same distinction applies on return:

\[\frac{d_{wave,BA}}{t_{BA}}=c,\qquad \frac{L}{t_{BA}}=c+v.\]

This is the terminology used throughout Path II:

  • wave propagation speed follows the travelling field feature;
  • message or arrival speed follows transfer from a specified emission endpoint to a specified moving reception endpoint;
  • local measurement refers to an operational measurement made locally by an observer;
  • coordinate one-way speed additionally depends on how distant clocks have been synchronized.

6. The round-trip asymmetry does not cancel arithmetically

The two message rates are \(c-v\) and \(c+v\). Their arithmetic mean is \(c\), but equal endpoint separations traversed at unequal rates must be combined through the travel times:

\[T=\frac{L}{c-v}+\frac{L}{c+v}.\]

Hence

\[T=\frac{2Lc}{c^2-v^2}.\]

The effective message-completion rate across the two endpoint separations is the harmonic mean:

\[v_{msg,round}=\frac{2}{\frac{1}{c-v}+\frac{1}{c+v}}=c\left(1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right).\]

This quantity is deliberately not called the wave speed. It is a property of the complete A→B→A message task with moving endpoints.

7. How the moving system receives its own time coordinate

Einstein’s next step is not to keep the stationary system’s clock synchronization for the moving rod. He defines a new time coordinate \(\tau\) for clocks at rest in the moving system by applying the same light-signal midpoint rule inside that system.

Schematically, if the moving-system signal leaves at \(\tau_0\), reflects at \(\tau_1\), and returns at \(\tau_2\), the synchronization condition is

\[\tau_1=\frac{\tau_0+\tau_2}{2}.\]

In the 1905 derivation this condition is inserted into the coordinate transformation. The resulting Lorentz time coordinate contains a position-dependent term:

\[\tau=\gamma\left(t-\frac{vx}{c^2}\right),\qquad \gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}.\]

That term changes which distant events the moving system labels simultaneous.

8. Why the one-way coordinate speed becomes \(c\)

Once the moving system has its own Einstein-synchronized clocks and Lorentz coordinates, a light ray satisfies

\[\xi^2+\eta^2+\zeta^2=c^2\tau^2.\]

So its coordinate speed in that system is again \(c\). In standard SR this is a central and consistent result: the relativity principle and Lorentz transformation preserve the light cone.

The portal’s question begins one level earlier. The same light signals are used to construct the distant time coordinate that is then used to report the one-way speed. Therefore the statement

\[v_{one-way}=c\]

should not be described as though it were obtained from two already synchronized distant clocks by a synchronization-independent one-way experiment.

9. What is measured, what is assigned, what is inferred

The distinctions can now be stated without rejecting the experimental success of clock comparisons:

  • emission and return at A can be timed by one local clock;
  • the B reflection is a real local event at B;
  • placing A and B readings on one common distant time scale requires synchronization;
  • Einstein synchronization assigns the midpoint relation;
  • in an external description, moving endpoints give unequal message encounter times \(L/(c-v)\) and \(L/(c+v)\);
  • in the moving system’s Einstein coordinates, the assigned one-way coordinate speeds are equal to \(c\).

10. The portal hypothesis

The alternative explored in Speed Without a Message is that a message retains the propagation history established at emission, while later receiver motion changes only where and when the receiver intersects that history.

In that reading,

\[c=\text{wave propagation rate},\]
\[c\pm v=\text{message-to-moving-receiver encounter rate}.\]

Einstein synchronization is then interpreted as a powerful coordinate construction that restores symmetric one-way coordinate speeds by changing the assignment of distant time.

This is not the standard interpretation of special relativity. It is the alternative physical reading being tested by this path.

11. Open obligation

A critique of synchronization is not enough by itself to replace special relativity. The alternative must specify an operational experiment whose result differs after all clocks, distances, Doppler effects and local measurements are defined without ambiguity.

The strongest test would avoid deriving its one-way result from the same synchronization rule whose physical meaning is in question. Until such a discriminating experiment is specified, the page establishes a distinction between wave propagation, message arrival and distant-time assignment — not a completed alternative theory.

Connection to the novel

Jan’s objection is not that the synchronization rule fails to coordinate clocks. It succeeds precisely because it supplies a common rule.

His question is narrower: when the same messenger is used to define distant time and then to state its own one-way speed, which part is observation and which part entered with the definition?
