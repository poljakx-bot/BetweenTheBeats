Path II · Theme 2

Sound, Motion and the Perceived Wavelength

Sound makes the distinction visible: wave speed, message arrival and the receiver’s local reconstruction are not one quantity.

Published essay

Three quantities to keep separate

The medium, the message and the receiver each define a different relation.

The wave propagates through the medium at \(c_s\). A moving receiver meets a particular message at \(c_s\pm u\). From successive local receptions it may reconstruct a received frequency and a corresponding reception wavelength whose product is again \(c_s\). These statements answer different questions.

Intuition

Sound gives us a medium we can see in the equations

Let sound propagate through still air at speed \(c_s\). That is the wave-propagation rate defined by the medium.

Now let a receiver move through the air. A marked pulse that chases a receding receiver closes the remaining distance at

\[c_s-u.\]

If the receiver moves towards the pulse, the distance closes at

\[c_s+u.\]

Those are message or arrival rates to that receiver. They do not require the sound wave itself to stop propagating through the air at \(c_s\).

A periodic wave adds a third question

A receiver does not only meet one marked pulse. It can register a sequence of equal-phase arrivals and measure their cadence. If the receiver moves away, the arrivals become less frequent; if it moves towards them, they become more frequent.

Path II then asks a deliberately operational question: if the received frequency belongs to the moving receiver, what wavelength belongs to the same sequence of local reception events?

Using the receiver’s displacement during one received period gives

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

This is not the same statement as saying that one marked message reaches the moving receiver at \(c_s\). The message-arrival rate remains \(c_s\pm u\).

Wave speed, message speed and the speed reconstructed from local periodic reception can coexist because they are defined by different event pairs.

The Argument

1. Begin in the medium frame

Let a stationary source generate a periodic sound wave in a stationary medium. Its source frequency and medium-frame wavelength satisfy

\[\lambda_0=\frac{c_s}{f_0}.\]

The wave propagates through the medium at

\[v_{wave}=c_s.\]

2. A moving receiver changes the reception cadence

Let the receiver move in the same direction as the wave at speed \(u\). The time between two successive equal-phase arrivals at the receiver is \(T_R=1/f_R\).

During that interval the receiver moves

\[d_R=uT_R.\]

The following front must cover the original spacing plus that new displacement:

\[c_sT_R=\lambda_0+uT_R.\]

Therefore

\[(c_s-u)T_R=\lambda_0,\]
\[f_R=\frac{c_s-u}{\lambda_0}=f_0\left(1-\frac{u}{c_s}\right).\]

3. Reconstruct the wavelength from the same reception events

The portal defines the reception wavelength \(\lambda_R\) as the propagation distance associated with those two successive local reception events. The first front is received, the receiver moves by \(d_R\), and the next front is received after traversing the original spacing plus that displacement:

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0+d_R=\lambda_0+uT_R.\]

Using the arrival equation above,

\[\lambda_R=c_sT_R=\frac{c_s}{f_R}.\]

Hence

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

4. Approach gives the reciprocal result

If the receiver moves towards the arriving wave, the next front has less distance to cover:

\[c_sT_R=\lambda_0-uT_R.\]

Therefore

\[f_R=f_0\left(1+\frac{u}{c_s}\right),\]

while the reception wavelength is

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0-uT_R=\frac{c_s}{f_R}.\]

Again,

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

5. The message rate remains different

Now forget the periodic train and follow one marked pulse. If the receiver begins a distance \(L\) ahead and recedes at \(u\),

\[c_st=L+ut.\]

so

\[t_{arr}=\frac{L}{c_s-u}.\]

With the Path II definition

\[v_{msg}=\frac{L}{t_{arr}},\]

the message-arrival rate is

\[\boxed{v_{msg}=c_s-u}.\]

There is no contradiction with \(f_R\lambda_R=c_s\), because the first quantity follows one message from source separation to reception, while the second is reconstructed from a periodic sequence of local phase receptions.

6. Source and receiver motion in one geometry

If the source moves through the medium at \(v_S\), successive fronts are emitted from different positions. The spacing created ahead of the source is

\[\lambda_M=(c_s-v_S)T_S=\frac{c_s-v_S}{f_S}.\]

If the receiver also moves in the same direction at \(v_R\), the next arrival satisfies

\[c_sT_R=\lambda_M+v_RT_R.\]

Thus

\[f_R=f_S\frac{c_s-v_R}{c_s-v_S}.\]

The corresponding reception wavelength is

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_M+v_RT_R,\]

and therefore

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

The source motion changes the spacing created during emission. The receiver motion changes the spatial interval associated with successive reception events. Both should be tracked explicitly instead of silently pairing quantities defined in different constructions.

Deep Notes

What is being compared?

Sound is useful because the medium gives an explicit propagation frame. Let the medium be at rest in coordinate system \(S\). A periodic disturbance propagates through it at speed \(c_s\). For a stationary source,

\[T_0=\frac{1}{f_0},\qquad \lambda_0=c_sT_0.\]

Now introduce a receiver moving parallel to the wave. Three different operational questions become available:

\[\boxed{\text{How fast does the wave propagate through the medium?}}\]
\[\boxed{\text{How fast does one marked message reach this moving receiver?}}\]
\[\boxed{\text{What wave speed is reconstructed from successive local receptions?}}\]

The central claim of this page is not that these answers must be numerically identical. It is that they must not be given the same name before the measurement procedure is specified.

Wave propagation through the medium

For a marked front,

\[x_F(t)=x_F(0)+c_st.\]

Therefore

\[v_{wave}=c_s.\]

This is the propagation speed relative to the medium.

One marked message to a moving receiver

Let a receiver start a distance \(L\) ahead and recede at speed \(u\):

\[x_R(t)=L+ut.\]

The arrival event satisfies

\[c_st=L+ut.\]

Hence

\[t_{arr}=\frac{L}{c_s-u}.\]

If message speed is defined by the initial source–receiver separation divided by arrival time,

\[\boxed{v_{msg}=c_s-u}.\]

For approach the sign reverses:

\[\boxed{v_{msg}=c_s+u}.\]

These are receiver-dependent arrival rates. They do not replace the medium-frame propagation speed \(c_s\).

Periodic reception and the received frequency

Now consider successive equal-phase fronts. At the instant one front reaches the receding receiver, the next front is one medium-frame wavelength \(\lambda_0\) behind. During the next received period \(T_R\), the receiver moves by

\[d_R=uT_R.\]

The following front must therefore propagate

\[c_sT_R=\lambda_0+d_R.\]

Substitution gives

\[(c_s-u)T_R=\lambda_0,\]

so the received frequency is

\[f_R=\frac{c_s-u}{\lambda_0}.\]

This is the usual moving-receiver Doppler cadence.

Two different wavelength constructions

A standard medium-frame description keeps \(\lambda_0\) as the simultaneous spatial separation of equal-phase fronts in the medium. With that convention,

\[f_R\lambda_0=c_s-u.\]

Path II asks a different operational question. The measured frequency \(f_R\) belongs to two successive reception events on the moving receiver’s history. What spatial interval belongs to those same two events?

The receiver moves by \(uT_R\) between them, so the propagation distance associated with that reception interval is

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0+uT_R.\]

Using the meeting condition,

\[\lambda_R=c_sT_R.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

The disagreement is therefore not algebraic. It is about which spatial quantity should be paired operationally with the receiver-measured frequency.

Why the distinction matters

The product

\[f_R\lambda_0=c_s-u\]

combines a frequency defined by the moving receiver with a wavelength defined as simultaneous front spacing in the medium frame. The portal asks whether that mixed construction should be described as the receiver’s measured wave speed.

The alternative reconstruction pairs quantities generated by the same reception interval:

\[f_R=\frac{1}{T_R},\qquad \lambda_R=c_sT_R,\]

giving

\[f_R\lambda_R=c_s.\]

This does not make the message-arrival rate \(c_s\). It only keeps local periodic reconstruction separate from source-to-receiver transport.

The two-sensor time-of-flight test

Place two sensors a fixed distance \(L\) apart on a vehicle moving at speed \(u\) through the medium. Let one marked sound pulse pass the rear sensor and then chase the front sensor.

During the transit time \(t\), the pulse moves through the medium by \(c_st\), while the front sensor moves by \(ut\). The meeting condition is

\[c_st=L+ut.\]

Thus

\[t=\frac{L}{c_s-u}\]

and the vehicle’s two-sensor time-of-flight ratio is

\[\frac{L}{t}=c_s-u.\]

This experiment measures message transit between two moving locations. It is not the same operation as reconstructing \(f_R\lambda_R\) from a periodic sequence at one receiver. The fact that the two procedures return different rates is the point: they answer different questions.

Source motion and receiver motion

For a source moving at \(v_S\), the medium-frame spacing generated ahead of it is

\[\lambda_M=\frac{c_s-v_S}{f_S}.\]

For a receiver moving at \(v_R\) in the same direction,

\[c_sT_R=\lambda_M+v_RT_R.\]

Hence

\[f_R=f_S\frac{c_s-v_R}{c_s-v_S}.\]

Using the same reception-event construction,

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_M+v_RT_R,\]

so

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

This formulation treats source and receiver motion through the same event geometry rather than changing the wave description for one while freezing it for the other.

Why this matters for the light argument

The sound example is not offered as proof that light has a material medium. Its purpose is conceptual. It shows that three quantities can coexist without contradiction:

\[\boxed{\text{wave propagation rate}}\]
\[\boxed{\text{message arrival rate to a moving receiver}}\]
\[\boxed{\text{speed reconstructed by a chosen local wave measurement}}\]

Path II then asks whether discussions of light and synchronization preserve the same distinction, or whether the invariant local value \(c\) is silently promoted into a universal statement about message arrival between arbitrary moving endpoints.

What is and is not claimed

The derivation does not dispute that sound propagates through a stationary medium at \(c_s\). It does not claim that a marked pulse has a time-of-flight speed \(c_s\) between two sensors moving through that medium. That experiment gives \(c_s\pm u\).

The narrower claim is that a periodic receiver can define a reception wavelength from the same reception interval that defines its received period, and that this construction yields \(f_R\lambda_R=c_s\). The physical disagreement with the textbook convention lies in what is meant by “the wavelength measured by the moving receiver”.
