Sound, Motion and the Reception Interval

Sound makes the distinction visible: the wave can propagate through the medium at \(c_s\), the receiver can meet its fronts at \(c_s\pm u\), and a local reconstruction can still return \(c_s\).

Published essay

The control case

What changes when the receiver moves?

Not the propagation law of the sound wave in the medium. What changes first is the interval between successive encounters with the moving receiver.

Intuition

The wave already has a propagation speed

Take a stationary sound source and stationary air. If the source emits at frequency \(f_0\), the wavelength in the medium is

\[\lambda_0=\frac{c_s}{f_0}.\]

The crests are separated by \(\lambda_0\) in the air and each crest propagates through the air at \(c_s\).

Move only the receiver

A receiver moving towards the incoming wave meets the crests more often. A receiver moving away meets them less often.

The wave has not acquired a new speed. The receiver has changed how quickly it crosses the already propagating sequence of crests.

Following successive crests at a moving receiver measures an encounter interval. It does not, by itself, remeasure the propagation speed of the wave.

Received frequency changes

For a receiver approaching at speed \(u\), successive crests close the receiver at \(c_s+u\). Therefore

\[f_R=f_0\left(1+\frac{u}{c_s}\right).\]

For recession,

\[f_R=f_0\left(1-\frac{u}{c_s}\right).\]

Why a local calculation can still give \(c_s\)

The receiver can define the spatial interval associated with one received period by asking how far sound itself propagates through the medium during that period:

\[\ell_R=c_sT_R=\frac{c_s}{f_R}.\]

Then

\[\boxed{f_R\ell_R=c_s}.\]

The increased frequency and the shortened reception interval compensate. The receiver can recover the propagation speed while the crest–receiver encounter rate remains \(c_s+u\).

The Argument

Start in the medium frame

Let the stationary source emit one crest every period

\[T_0=\frac{1}{f_0}.\]

During one source period, sound propagates

\[\lambda_0=c_sT_0=\frac{c_s}{f_0}.\]

This is the spacing of successive crests in the medium.

Approaching receiver

Let the receiver move towards the source at speed \(u\). The distance between an incoming crest and the receiver closes at

\[v_{enc}=c_s+u.\]

The time between successive crest encounters is therefore

\[T_R=\frac{\lambda_0}{c_s+u}.\]

Hence

\[f_R=\frac{1}{T_R}=\frac{c_s+u}{\lambda_0}=f_0\left(1+\frac{u}{c_s}\right).\]

Receding receiver

For recession,

\[v_{enc}=c_s-u,\qquad T_R=\frac{\lambda_0}{c_s-u},\]

so

\[f_R=f_0\left(1-\frac{u}{c_s}\right).\]

Do not rename \(\lambda_0\)

The medium-frame crest spacing \(\lambda_0\) has not changed merely because the receiver moved. What changed is the time required for the moving receiver to encounter that spacing.

If one writes

\[f_R\lambda_0=c_s\pm u,\]

the right-hand side is the encounter rate because \(f_R\) is a receiver sampling rate while \(\lambda_0\) is still the crest spacing defined in the medium.

Mix a receiver frequency with a medium-frame wavelength and the product gives the closing rate, not a new propagation speed.

A receiver-defined interval is different

Define instead

\[\ell_R\equiv c_sT_R=\frac{c_s}{f_R}.\]

This is the distance sound propagates through the medium during one received period. It is not the unchanged crest spacing \(\lambda_0\).

Now

\[\boxed{f_R\ell_R=c_s}.\]

The two products answer different questions because the spatial intervals are different.

The control result

\[\boxed{v_{prop}=c_s,\qquad v_{enc}=c_s\pm u,\qquad f_R\ell_R=c_s.}\]

No paradox is required. Propagation, encounter and local reconstruction have simply been kept separate.

Deep Notes

Sound is useful here because the medium gives the propagation law an unambiguous reference. In stationary air, a marked acoustic disturbance propagates at the sound speed \(c_s\) relative to that air. Moving the receiver does not alter that wave equation. It alters the rate at which the receiver samples the travelling spatial pattern.

This makes it possible to see exactly where two different products can arise. If the received frequency is combined with the original wavelength in the medium, the product is a crest–receiver encounter rate. If the received frequency is combined with the distance sound itself travels during one received period, the product returns the propagation speed.

The distinction is not semantic. The two “wavelength-like” distances refer to different constructions.

The medium-frame wave train

For a stationary monochromatic source,

\[y(x,t)=A\cos(kx-\omega t),\]

with

\[c_s=\frac{\omega}{k}=f_0\lambda_0.\]

At any fixed medium-frame time, adjacent equal-phase crests are separated by \(\lambda_0\).

Sample the field along a moving receiver trajectory

Let an approaching receiver follow

\[x_R(t)=x_0-ut.\]

Substituting the receiver trajectory into the wave gives

\[y_R(t)=A\cos\!\left(kx_0-(\omega+ku)t\right).\]

The receiver therefore samples an angular frequency

\[\omega_R=\omega+ku.\]

Using \(\omega=kc_s\),

\[\omega_R=k(c_s+u),\]

or

\[f_R=f_0\left(1+\frac{u}{c_s}\right).\]

This derivation makes the geometry explicit: the change comes from evaluating the same propagating field along a different receiver trajectory.

What \(f_R\lambda_0\) actually measures

Because \(\lambda_0=2\pi/k\),

\[f_R\lambda_0=c_s+u\]

for approach.

This does not mean the sound wave now propagates at \(c_s+u\). The receiver crosses a spatial phase interval \(\lambda_0\) at the relative rate \(c_s+u\). The product combines a receiver encounter frequency with a medium-frame phase spacing.

The reception interval gives another spatial quantity

One received period is

\[T_R=\frac{1}{f_R}.\]

During that same time, the sound wave propagates through the medium by

\[\ell_R=c_sT_R.\]

Therefore

\[f_R\ell_R=c_s.\]

For approach,

\[\ell_R=\lambda_0\frac{c_s}{c_s+u},\]

and for recession,

\[\ell_R=\lambda_0\frac{c_s}{c_s-u}.\]

So \(\ell_R\) is not the fixed crest-to-crest spacing already present in the medium. It is the propagation distance associated with the receiver's shorter or longer sampling period.

Why tracking peaks can hide the distinction

If an experiment follows the times at which successive peaks reach a moving object, it is measuring intersections between a spatial wave pattern and a moving trajectory. Dividing the fixed crest spacing by those encounter intervals naturally produces \(c_s\pm u\).

The wave itself enters by setting how the pattern moves through the medium. The extra \(\pm u\) enters because the receiver also moves through that pattern.

Calling the result “the measured speed of sound for that receiver” silently changes the object being measured from propagation to closing geometry.

A standing pattern makes the sampling issue even clearer

Consider a standing acoustic pattern. Its nodes and antinodes are stationary in the medium even though the field oscillates in time. A receiver moving through that pattern crosses spatial maxima at a rate set by its own motion relative to the pattern.

No one would identify that node-crossing rate with the propagation speed of sound. It is a sampling rate produced by motion through spatial structure.

A travelling wave adds the pattern speed \(c_s\); the general lesson remains the same: peak encounter is determined by relative trajectory, not by propagation alone.

Why this matters for light

For sound, nobody needs spacetime transformation to explain the coexistence of

\[c_s,\qquad c_s\pm u,\qquad f_R\ell_R=c_s.\]

They are three operational statements about one wave and one moving receiver.

Path II uses this as a control before returning to light. The question is not whether sound and light share the same physical medium. They do not in standard physics. The question is whether the distinction between propagation and encounter remains logically valid when the travelling field is electromagnetic.

The working distinction

\[\boxed{\text{medium-frame wavelength}\neq\text{receiver-defined reception interval}.}\]

Once those distances are kept distinct, the apparently conflicting products \(f_R\lambda_0=c_s\pm u\) and \(f_R\ell_R=c_s\) describe different quantities rather than contradictory measurements.
