Path II · Theme 2

Sound, Motion and the Moving Receiver

The wave propagates through the medium. The receiver moves through that propagation. Their encounter is a third quantity.

Published essay

Why sound?

Because here propagation and reception can be separated without ambiguity about the medium.

In still air, a sound front propagates through the medium at \(c_s\). A moving receiver changes the place and time of reception. The central question is whether the rate at which their separation closes should be given the same physical name as the propagation rate of the wave itself.

Intuition

Three quantities, three questions

Take still air and a stationary source. A crest of the sound wave moves through the air. Call its position \(x_W(t)\). If the speed of sound in that medium is \(c_s\), then

\[\boxed{\frac{dx_W}{dt}=c_s}.\]

Now let a receiver move in the same direction at speed \(u\), with position \(x_R(t)\).

The distance between receiver and crest is

\[D(t)=x_R(t)-x_W(t).\]

For a receding receiver, that distance closes at

\[\boxed{-\frac{dD}{dt}=c_s-u}.\]

These are not two answers to the same derivative. The first follows the wave. The second follows the separation between the wave and another moving object.

Wave propagation and wave–receiver closing rate are different physical quantities, even when the same reception event connects them.

The frozen-wave trap

A snapshot of a periodic wave shows crests separated by \(\lambda_0\). That snapshot is useful for counting which crest comes next, but the photograph has removed the wave's motion from view.

If the spatial pattern is then treated as though it were the physical wave, the moving receiver appears to sweep through a set of fixed phase markers. The calculation naturally becomes one of relative encounter or closing geometry.

The actual wave is not frozen. Every crest continues to move:

\[x_n(t)=x_{n0}+c_st.\]

The frozen-wave picture is useful for counting encounters, but dangerous if the resulting encounter rate is then renamed as the propagation speed of the wave.

What the receiver changes

Different receiver motions produce different reception histories. A receiver moving away encounters successive crests less often; one moving towards them encounters them more often.

That changes the received frequency. It does not require the same sound wave to acquire a different propagation history through the same air.

The Argument

1. Define propagation first

Let the medium and source be at rest. A marked sound crest follows

\[x_W(t)=x_{W0}+c_st.\]

Its propagation speed through the medium is therefore

\[\boxed{v_{prop}\equiv\frac{dx_W}{dt}=c_s}.\]

This definition follows the trajectory of the wave feature itself.

2. Add a moving receiver

Let the receiver move in the same direction at constant speed \(u\):

\[x_R(t)=x_{R0}+ut.\]

Define their instantaneous separation by

\[D(t)=x_R(t)-x_W(t).\]

Then

\[\frac{dD}{dt}=u-c_s,\]

so the rate at which the crest closes on the receding receiver is

\[\boxed{v_{close}\equiv-\frac{dD}{dt}=c_s-u}.\]

For an approaching receiver the sign reverses and the magnitude of the closing rate is \(c_s+u\).

3. The two derivatives must not be silently exchanged

\[\boxed{\frac{dx_W}{dt}=c_s\qquad\text{but}\qquad-\frac{d(x_R-x_W)}{dt}=c_s-u.}\]

The first asks how the wave moves through the medium. The second asks how quickly the distance between two moving trajectories disappears.

The arithmetic is not in dispute. The physical question changes when the numerator changes.

4. Why the Doppler calculation produces \(c_s-u\)

For a periodic wave, let two neighbouring crests be separated in the medium by

\[\lambda_0=\frac{c_s}{f_0}.\]

Choose \(t=0\) when one crest reaches the receiver. The next crest is then at

\[x_2(0)=-\lambda_0,\]

and it continues to propagate:

\[x_2(t)=-\lambda_0+c_st.\]

Put the receiver at

\[x_R(t)=ut.\]

The next reception occurs when the two moving trajectories intersect:

\[-\lambda_0+c_sT_R=uT_R.\]

Hence

\[(c_s-u)T_R=\lambda_0,\qquad \boxed{T_R=\frac{\lambda_0}{c_s-u}}.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{f_R=\frac{1}{T_R}=f_0\left(1-\frac{u}{c_s}\right)}.\]

This is the rate at which the moving receiver encounters successive propagating crests. The factor \(c_s-u\) enters because it is the closing rate of those two trajectories.

5. Why the frozen-wave picture is tempting

A snapshot correctly records that successive crests are separated by \(\lambda_0\). But a snapshot does not contain the temporal statement

\[\frac{dx_W}{dt}=c_s.\]

If the snapshot is mentally promoted into the wave itself, the receiver seems to move through a stationary phase pattern. The calculation then focuses on how quickly receiver and phase markers meet.

Restoring the crest trajectory shows what the calculation actually contains: both the crest and receiver move, and \(c_s-u\) is obtained from their separation.

6. What a receiver directly records

A single microphone records a local pressure history as a function of its own time. From that record it can obtain a reception frequency \(f_R\). It does not, from one point alone, directly observe a spatial propagation speed.

Any speed measurement therefore combines multiple events with a chosen spatial distance. That is precisely where the distinction matters: the path travelled by the wave and the changing separation to a moving receiver are not the same distance.

7. The control-case conclusion

\[\boxed{\text{propagation through medium}\neq\text{wave–receiver closing rate}\neq\text{reception cadence}.}\]

All receivers may have different encounter histories while the same wave continues to obey the same propagation law through the same medium.

8. Why this belongs before light

Sound makes the medium physically explicit. It therefore lets us separate the messenger's propagation from the moving endpoint of a message before asking whether those terms remain equally distinct when the messenger is light.

The question carried forward is not whether the Doppler arithmetic works. It does. The question is what quantity has actually been measured when an arrival or closing rate is called a propagation speed.

Deep Notes

Start with the physical control case. A sound wave propagates through a material medium while a receiver may move through that medium. Throughout this page, \(c_s\) means the propagation speed of sound relative to the stationary medium, and \(x,t\) are medium-frame coordinates unless another description is stated explicitly. We use a one-dimensional, uniform, stationary and approximately non-dispersive medium so that one marked phase feature can be followed with constant medium-relative speed \(c_s\).

1. Medium-frame wave propagation

Let the medium and source be stationary. A crest is a tracked phase feature of the disturbance, not a parcel of air travelling from source to receiver. The molecules of the medium oscillate locally; the marked phase feature propagates through them.

For that one-dimensional marked crest, write

\[x_W(t)=x_{W0}+c_st.\]

The medium-relative propagation speed is therefore

\[\boxed{v_{prop}=\frac{dx_W}{dt}=c_s}.\]

For a periodic source with frequency \(f_0\),

\[T_0=\frac1{f_0},\qquad \lambda_0=c_sT_0=\frac{c_s}{f_0}.\]

Here \(\lambda_0\) is the simultaneous spatial separation of equal-phase fronts in the medium frame.

2. Receiver trajectory

Let a receiver recede in the same direction at speed \(u\):

\[x_R(t)=x_{R0}+ut.\]

The separation between receiver and a selected crest is

\[D(t)=x_R(t)-x_W(t).\]

Differentiating gives

\[\frac{dD}{dt}=u-c_s.\]

Thus the closing rate is

\[\boxed{v_{close}=c_s-u}.\]

This quantity depends on both trajectories. It is not the derivative of the wave trajectory alone.

3. The decisive distinction

\[\boxed{\underbrace{\frac{dx_W}{dt}}_{\text{wave propagation}}=c_s}\]
\[\boxed{\underbrace{-\frac{d(x_R-x_W)}{dt}}_{\text{closing / encounter rate}}=c_s-u}.\]

The two expressions can participate in the same reception event without representing the same physical quantity. One follows the messenger. The other follows the changing separation between messenger and moving endpoint.

4. One reception-to-reception interval

Set the first reception event at \(t=0,x=0\). At that instant the receiver and crest 1 coincide. Crest 2 is one wavelength behind:

\[x_2(0)=-\lambda_0.\]

After that instant crest 2 does not remain there. It propagates:

\[\boxed{x_2(t)=-\lambda_0+c_st}.\]

The receiver simultaneously moves:

\[x_R(t)=ut.\]

The next reception is their intersection:

\[-\lambda_0+c_sT_R=uT_R.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{T_R=\frac{\lambda_0}{c_s-u}}.\]

The received frequency is

\[\boxed{f_R=\frac1{T_R}=\frac{c_s-u}{\lambda_0}=f_0\left(1-\frac{u}{c_s}\right)}.\]

No crest has been stopped in this derivation. The quantity \(c_s-u\) appears because the initial phase separation \(\lambda_0\) is closed by two moving trajectories.

Multiplying the receiver-defined temporal cadence by the medium-frame spatial phase separation gives

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_0=c_s-u}.\]

This number is mathematically correct. But notice what has been combined: \(f_R\) is defined by successive receptions at the moving receiver, while \(\lambda_0\) is defined by simultaneous phase spacing in the medium frame. Their product therefore reconstructs the encounter rate between receiver and phase pattern.

5. Check the actual distance travelled by the crest

During the same interval \(T_R\), crest 2 travels through the medium by

\[d_W=c_sT_R.\]

Hence

\[\boxed{\frac{d_W}{T_R}=c_s}.\]

Meanwhile the receiver moves by \(uT_R\), and the geometry also gives

\[d_W=\lambda_0+uT_R.\]

Thus \((c_s-u)T_R=\lambda_0\) and \(d_W/T_R=c_s\) are simultaneously true because \(\lambda_0\) and \(d_W\) are different distances.

The same reception interval supports two correct quotients: \(\lambda_0/T_R=c_s-u\) describes closing of the initial phase separation, while \(d_W/T_R=c_s\) describes the actual medium-frame propagation of the crest.

6. Why a frozen snapshot can produce the wrong intuition

A spatial snapshot shows the phase fronts but suppresses their time evolution. It preserves

\[\lambda_0=\text{distance between neighbouring equal-phase fronts at one instant},\]

while hiding

\[x_n(t)=x_{n0}+c_st.\]

If that photograph is then used as though it were the full physical wave, the remaining visible motion belongs to the receiver. The mental picture becomes a receiver sweeping through fixed markers.

That picture can still count encounters correctly when the missing wave motion is restored algebraically, but it invites a naming error: the relative rate governing those encounters can be mistaken for the propagation rate of the wave.

A snapshot preserves phase spacing, not propagation. Encounter geometry is not a substitute for the trajectory of the messenger.

7. Approaching receiver

For approach, choose the receiver velocity with the opposite sign. The magnitude of the closing rate becomes

\[\boxed{v_{close}=c_s+u}.\]

The reception interval and frequency are then

\[T_R=\frac{\lambda_0}{c_s+u},\qquad \boxed{f_R=f_0\left(1+\frac{u}{c_s}\right)}.\]

The wave itself still follows the same medium-frame propagation law \(dx_W/dt=c_s\).

8. What "measuring sound speed" can mean

There are several operationally different questions that ordinary language may compress into one phrase.

Propagation: how fast does a marked wave feature move through the medium?

\[v_{prop}=c_s.\]

Closing or encounter: how fast does the separation between that feature and a moving receiver change?

\[v_{close}=c_s\pm u.\]

Reception cadence: how often does that receiver encounter successive equal-phase features?

\[f_R=\frac1{T_R}.\]

A coordinate description may legitimately speak of the wave's velocity relative to a moving receiver. For example, with the Galilean receiver coordinate \(x'=x-ut\), the same crest satisfies

\[\frac{dx'_W}{dt}=c_s-u.\]

That is a legitimate statement about relative velocity. It is not a statement that the propagation process through the same medium has changed from \(c_s\) to \(c_s-u\). The point of this page is to keep those two physical questions named separately.

9. The message version

For a single marked pulse beginning a distance \(L\) behind a receding receiver, take

\[x_W(t)=c_st,\qquad x_R(t)=L+ut.\]

Reception occurs at

\[c_st=L+ut,\]

so

\[t_{msg}=\frac{L}{c_s-u}.\]

If one forms

\[\frac{L}{t_{msg}}=c_s-u,\]

the numerator is the initial source–receiver separation. It is not the distance travelled by the pulse. The pulse actually travels

\[d_W=c_st_{msg},\qquad \frac{d_W}{t_{msg}}=c_s.\]

This is the same distinction in message form.

10. A numerical check

Take a medium in which

\[c_s=340\ \mathrm{m/s},\qquad u=40\ \mathrm{m/s},\qquad \lambda_0=3.4\ \mathrm{m}.\]

The receiver recedes in the direction of propagation. The interval until the next crest reaches it is

\[T_R=\frac{3.4}{340-40}\approx0.01133\ \mathrm{s}.\]

Using the initial crest separation gives

\[\frac{\lambda_0}{T_R}\approx300\ \mathrm{m/s}=c_s-u.\]

But during that same interval the crest itself travels

\[d_W=340(0.01133)\approx3.853\ \mathrm{m}.\]

The receiver travels

\[uT_R=40(0.01133)\approx0.453\ \mathrm{m},\]

so the geometry closes exactly as expected:

\[3.853\approx3.4+0.453.\]

Following the crest's actual path gives

\[\boxed{\frac{3.853}{0.01133}\approx340\ \mathrm{m/s}=c_s}.\]

The \(300\ \mathrm{m/s}\) result belongs to closing the initial \(3.4\ \mathrm{m}\) phase separation. The \(340\ \mathrm{m/s}\) result belongs to the distance actually propagated by the crest through the medium.

11. Boundary of the conclusion

\[\boxed{\text{wave trajectory}\neq\text{wave–receiver separation}\neq\text{reception history}.}\]

Sound establishes that those three objects can be cleanly separated when a propagation medium is explicit. It does not by itself settle how light must be described. It supplies the control case and the vocabulary needed for the next question.
