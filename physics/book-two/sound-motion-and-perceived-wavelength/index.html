Sound, Motion and the Perceived Wavelength

Receiver motion changes both the frequency and the wavelength physically perceived during reception; cₛ±u remains the arrival rate at a marked front.

Intuition

Sound provides a controlled example because its propagation can be separated from the motion of both source and receiver.

When a receiver moves, it does not merely register a changed frequency. It physically encounters a changed wavelength in its own sequence of reception. Here perceived is used in the operational physical sense: the wavelength encountered and registered by the moving receiver, not a subjective impression.

The receiver’s motion changes frequency and perceived wavelength in inverse proportions:

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

The speed (c_s) remains the propagation speed of the sound wave. The quantities (c_s\pm u) describe only how rapidly the receiver and a marked front approach one another.

The Argument

A receiver moving with the sound

Let sound propagate through a stationary medium at speed \(c_s\). A stationary source produces frequency \(f_0\) and medium-frame wavelength

\[\lambda_0=\frac{c_s}{f_0}.\]

Now let the receiver move in the same direction as the sound at speed \(u\). Let

\[T_R=\frac{1}{f_R}\]

be the time between two successive arrivals at that receiver. During this interval the receiver moves a real distance

\[d_R=uT_R=\frac{u}{f_R}.\]

At the first arrival, the next equal-phase front is one medium-frame wavelength \(\lambda_0\) behind. Because the receiver continues moving away, that next front must cover the original separation plus the receiver's displacement:

\[c_sT_R=\lambda_0+uT_R.\]

Therefore

\[(c_s-u)T_R=\lambda_0,\]
\[T_R=\frac{\lambda_0}{c_s-u},\qquad f_R=\frac{c_s-u}{\lambda_0}=f_0\left(1-\frac{u}{c_s}\right).\]

The perceived wavelength is now obtained from the same geometry. It is the original front spacing plus the distance travelled by the receiver between the two arrivals:

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0+d_R=\lambda_0+\frac{u}{f_R}.\]

Multiplying by the received frequency gives

\[f_R\lambda_R=f_R\lambda_0+u.\]

But the arrival equation already gave

\[f_R\lambda_0=c_s-u.\]

Hence

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=(c_s-u)+u=c_s}.\]

Equivalently, substituting the received frequency into the geometric expression for wavelength gives

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0+u\frac{\lambda_0}{c_s-u}=\frac{c_s\lambda_0}{c_s-u}=\frac{\lambda_0}{1-u/c_s}.\]

A receiver moving towards the sound

Let \(u\) now denote the positive magnitude of motion towards the arriving wave. During one received period the receiver moves towards the next front by

\[d_R=uT_R=\frac{u}{f_R}.\]

The next front therefore covers less than the original separation:

\[c_sT_R=\lambda_0-uT_R.\]

Thus

\[(c_s+u)T_R=\lambda_0,\qquad f_R=\frac{c_s+u}{\lambda_0}=f_0\left(1+\frac{u}{c_s}\right).\]

The perceived wavelength is shortened by exactly the distance the receiver travels towards the arriving front:

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0-d_R=\lambda_0-\frac{u}{f_R}.\]

Therefore

\[f_R\lambda_R=f_R\lambda_0-u=(c_s+u)-u=c_s.\]
\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

Source and receiver both moving

Let the source move in the direction of propagation at speed \(v_S\). During one source period \(T_S=1/f_S\), the first front advances by \(c_sT_S\) while the source advances by \(v_ST_S\). The spacing created ahead of the source is therefore

\[\lambda_M=(c_s-v_S)T_S=\frac{c_s-v_S}{f_S}.\]

A receiver moving in the same direction at speed \(v_R\) travels a distance \(v_RT_R\) during one received period. The next front must cover the emitted spacing plus that displacement:

\[c_sT_R=\lambda_M+v_RT_R.\]

Hence

\[f_R=\frac{c_s-v_R}{\lambda_M}=f_S\frac{c_s-v_R}{c_s-v_S}.\]

The wavelength perceived by the moving receiver follows from the same encounter geometry:

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_M+v_RT_R=\lambda_M+\frac{v_R}{f_R}.\]

Multiplying gives

\[f_R\lambda_R=f_R\lambda_M+v_R=(c_s-v_R)+v_R=c_s.\]
\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

Deep Notes

This section is a complete technical presentation. It derives the perceived wavelength from the receiver's actual displacement between two successive arrivals. It does not begin by defining \(\lambda_R=c_sT_R\), because doing so would insert the desired result before it had been demonstrated.

1. Definitions and physical setup

Let the medium be at rest in coordinate system \(S\). Sound propagates through it at speed \(c_s\). A stationary source emits a periodic disturbance with period, frequency and medium-frame wavelength

\[T_0=\frac{1}{f_0},\qquad \lambda_0=c_sT_0=\frac{c_s}{f_0}.\]

The quantity \(\lambda_0\) is the separation between successive equal-phase fronts for an observer at rest in the medium. Now introduce a receiver moving parallel to the wave. Its received period is

\[T_R=\frac{1}{f_R}.\]

During this measured interval the receiver travels a physical distance. That displacement is the missing geometrical term when one asks what wavelength the moving receiver actually encounters.

Three quantities must remain distinct:

  • \(c_s\): propagation speed of the sound disturbance through the medium;
  • \(c_s\pm u\): closing rate between a labelled front and the moving receiver;
  • \(\lambda_R\): wavelength perceived through the receiver's two successive local arrival events.

2. Receiver moving away from the wave

Let the receiver move in the same direction as the wave with speed \(u>0\). At the instant one front reaches it, the next equal-phase front is one medium-frame wavelength \(\lambda_0\) behind.

During the next received period \(T_R\), the receiver moves forward by

\[d_R=uT_R=\frac{u}{f_R}.\]

The following front therefore does not merely cross the original separation \(\lambda_0\). It must also cover the additional distance created by the receiver's motion. The meeting condition is

\[c_sT_R=\lambda_0+d_R=\lambda_0+uT_R.\]

Collecting the time terms gives

\[(c_s-u)T_R=\lambda_0.\]

Hence the received period and frequency are

\[T_R=\frac{\lambda_0}{c_s-u},\]
\[f_R=\frac{1}{T_R}=\frac{c_s-u}{\lambda_0}.\]

Using \(\lambda_0=c_s/f_0\), this becomes the usual receiver Doppler result

\[f_R=f_0\left(1-\frac{u}{c_s}\right).\]

Now derive the perceived wavelength without using \(c_sT_R\) as its definition. The moving receiver encounters the second front only after advancing by \(d_R\). The separation physically traversed between its two arrival events is therefore

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0+d_R.\]

Since \(d_R=u/f_R\),

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0+\frac{u}{f_R}.\]

Multiply by \(f_R\):

\[f_R\lambda_R=f_R\lambda_0+u.\]

The arrival equation gave

\[f_R\lambda_0=c_s-u.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=(c_s-u)+u=c_s}.\]

The inverse change of wavelength can also be written explicitly:

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0+u\frac{\lambda_0}{c_s-u},\]
\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0\left(1+\frac{u}{c_s-u}\right),\]
\[\boxed{\lambda_R=\frac{c_s\lambda_0}{c_s-u}=\frac{\lambda_0}{1-u/c_s}}.\]

The receiver therefore records a lower frequency and a longer perceived wavelength. Both changes arise from the same motion and cancel in the wave-speed product.

3. Receiver moving towards the wave

Let \(u>0\) now denote the magnitude of motion towards the arriving fronts. During one received period the receiver moves towards the next front by

\[d_R=uT_R=\frac{u}{f_R}.\]

The following front now travels less than the original medium-frame separation:

\[c_sT_R=\lambda_0-d_R=\lambda_0-uT_R.\]

Thus

\[(c_s+u)T_R=\lambda_0,\]
\[T_R=\frac{\lambda_0}{c_s+u},\qquad f_R=\frac{c_s+u}{\lambda_0}=f_0\left(1+\frac{u}{c_s}\right).\]

The perceived wavelength is shortened by the distance the receiver travels towards the next front:

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0-d_R=\lambda_0-\frac{u}{f_R}.\]

Therefore

\[f_R\lambda_R=f_R\lambda_0-u=(c_s+u)-u=c_s.\]
\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

Its explicit form is

\[\boxed{\lambda_R=\frac{\lambda_0}{1+u/c_s}}.\]

4. Why the result is not assumed

The derivation does not start from \(\lambda_R=c_s/f_R\) or \(\lambda_R=c_sT_R\). Either expression would force the product \(f_R\lambda_R\) to equal \(c_s\) by definition.

Instead, it begins with two independently stated pieces of geometry:

  • the next front is initially separated by the medium-frame distance \(\lambda_0\);
  • the receiver moves by the measured distance \(d_R=u/f_R\) between the two arrivals.

The perceived wavelength is then the original separation corrected by that actual receiver displacement:

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0\pm\frac{u}{f_R}.\]

Only after this independent geometrical construction is the product evaluated.

5. Propagation speed and arrival rate

For the receiver moving away, retaining the medium-frame wavelength while using the receiver's changed frequency gives

\[f_R\lambda_0=c_s-u.\]

For motion towards the wave it gives

\[f_R\lambda_0=c_s+u.\]

These are not new propagation speeds of sound. They are closing rates between a labelled front and a moving receiver. They arise because a temporal quantity measured by the receiver is being paired with a spatial separation held fixed in the medium frame.

When the receiver's displacement is included in the wavelength it physically encounters, the distinction becomes

\[\boxed{c_s\pm u=\text{arrival rate at a labelled front}},\]
\[\boxed{c_s=f_R\lambda_R=\text{propagation speed reconstructed from the received wave}}.\]

6. Moving source: creation of the front spacing

Let the source move in the propagation direction at speed \(v_S\) and emit with period \(T_S=1/f_S\). During one source period, the earlier front advances by \(c_sT_S\), while the source itself advances by \(v_ST_S\). The spacing created ahead of the source is

\[\lambda_M=(c_s-v_S)T_S=\frac{c_s-v_S}{f_S}.\]

Behind the source the spacing is \((c_s+v_S)/f_S\). Source motion changes where successive fronts are generated. Receiver motion changes how a previously generated sequence is encountered.

7. Source and receiver moving in the same direction

Let a receiver move in the propagation direction at speed \(v_R\). At one arrival, the next front is separated by \(\lambda_M\). During the received period \(T_R\), the receiver advances by \(v_RT_R\). The next front must therefore satisfy

\[c_sT_R=\lambda_M+v_RT_R.\]

Hence

\[(c_s-v_R)T_R=\lambda_M,\]
\[f_R=\frac{c_s-v_R}{\lambda_M}=f_S\frac{c_s-v_R}{c_s-v_S}.\]

The receiver-perceived wavelength is obtained geometrically:

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_M+v_RT_R=\lambda_M+\frac{v_R}{f_R}.\]

Therefore

\[f_R\lambda_R=f_R\lambda_M+v_R=(c_s-v_R)+v_R=c_s.\]
\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

8. What “perceived” means

Perceived is used in a physical, operational sense. It does not refer to a subjective auditory impression. It denotes the wavelength physically encountered and registered through two successive arrival events by a receiver that changes position between those events.

The receiver does not invent a new wavelength by definition. Its motion changes the geometrical separation that the next front must traverse before the next local arrival can occur.

9. Result and scope

The medium-frame wavelength \(\lambda_0\) remains a valid description of front spacing in the rest frame of the medium. The claim is that it cannot be paired with a moving receiver's frequency and then called that receiver's measured wavelength without omitting the receiver's displacement between arrivals.

Once that displacement is included, frequency and perceived wavelength change together, while their product retains the propagation speed:

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

Connection to the novel

Jan uses sound not as an analogy that must be discarded before light, but as a diagnostic case. It separates propagation from collision with a front and forces frequency and perceived wavelength to be treated as one physical measurement.

Once that is done, invariance is no longer unique to light.
