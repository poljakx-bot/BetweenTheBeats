Sound, Motion and the Received Wavelength

Sound is the control case: the wave really propagates through a medium, while the receiver moves through that already moving wave.

Published essay

Why sound?

Because here the distinction between propagation and reception is physically visible.

In still air, the sound wave propagates through the medium at \(c_s\). A moving receiver does not stop that propagation; it changes where successive parts of the moving wave meet the receiver. That makes sound a clean control case before the same vocabulary is used for light.

Intuition

The wave is already moving

Take a source and still air at rest together. The source produces a periodic sound wave. In the air, successive equal-phase parts of the wave are separated by

\[\lambda_0=\frac{c_s}{f_0}.\]

Now let a receiver move in the same direction as the wave.

At the instant one crest reaches the receiver, the next crest is \(\lambda_0\) behind. Before that next crest arrives, two things happen at once: the crest propagates through the air, and the receiver moves farther away.

The receiver is not moving through a frozen pattern. It is moving inside a pattern that is itself propagating.

Receding receiver

During the received period \(T_R\), the receiver moves by \(uT_R\). The next crest must therefore propagate not merely the original \(\lambda_0\), but

\[\boxed{\lambda_R=\lambda_0+uT_R}.\]

At the same time, because that crest propagates through the medium at \(c_s\),

\[\lambda_R=c_sT_R.\]

Combining the two gives

\[c_sT_R=\lambda_0+uT_R,\]

so

\[f_R=f_0\left(1-\frac{u}{c_s}\right).\]

and, independently from the geometry above,

\[\boxed{\lambda_R=\lambda_0\frac{c_s}{c_s-u}}.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

For recession, the receiver gets a lower frequency and a larger receiver-side wavelength.

What \(\lambda_R\) means here

It does not mean that the simultaneous crest spacing in the air has physically stretched. A snapshot in the medium frame still gives \(\lambda_0\).

Here \(\lambda_R\) is the propagation distance belonging to one complete received cycle: the distance travelled by the next equal-phase part of the moving wave between two successive equal-phase encounters with the moving receiver.

The Argument

1. Begin with unquestioned wave propagation

In a stationary medium, sound propagates at \(c_s\). For a stationary source of frequency \(f_0\),

\[T_0=\frac{1}{f_0},\qquad \lambda_0=c_sT_0=\frac{c_s}{f_0}.\]

This \(\lambda_0\) is the simultaneous spacing of equal-phase fronts in the medium frame.

2. Let the receiver move through that propagating wave

Suppose the receiver recedes at speed \(u\). At one reception event, crest 1 is at the receiver and crest 2 is \(\lambda_0\) behind it.

During the interval \(T_R\) until crest 2 reaches the receiver, the receiver moves an additional distance \(uT_R\). Therefore crest 2 must travel

\[\boxed{\lambda_R=\lambda_0+uT_R}.\]

But crest 2 is a part of the sound wave, so during the same interval it propagates through the medium by

\[\boxed{\lambda_R=c_sT_R}.\]

3. Derive the received frequency

Equating the two expressions for the distance travelled by crest 2,

\[c_sT_R=\lambda_0+uT_R.\]

Hence

\[(c_s-u)T_R=\lambda_0,\]

so

\[T_R=\frac{\lambda_0}{c_s-u},\]

and

\[\boxed{f_R=\frac{1}{T_R}=f_0\left(1-\frac{u}{c_s}\right)}.\]

4. Derive the received wavelength from geometry, not from \(c_s/f_R\)

Return to

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0+uT_R.\]

Substitute \(T_R=\lambda_0/(c_s-u)\):

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0+u\frac{\lambda_0}{c_s-u}.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{\lambda_R=\lambda_0\frac{c_s}{c_s-u}}.\]

The receiver-side wavelength has been obtained from the moving-wave geometry itself.

5. The product follows

Now multiply the two independently obtained receiver quantities:

\[f_R\lambda_R=f_0\left(1-\frac{u}{c_s}\right)\lambda_0\frac{c_s}{c_s-u}.\]

Using \(f_0\lambda_0=c_s\),

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

The receiver has changed both the cadence of its encounters and the propagation distance belonging to one received cycle. The wave itself has continued to propagate through the medium at \(c_s\).

6. Why multiplying \(f_R\) by \(\lambda_0\) gives something else

If the receiver frequency is instead paired with the simultaneous medium-frame crest spacing,

\[f_R\lambda_0=c_s-u.\]

That quotient describes the closing rate between the receding receiver and the repeating wave pattern. It mixes a receiver-defined temporal interval with a medium-frame snapshot distance.

Both equations are correct. They answer different geometric questions.

7. Approaching receiver

If the receiver moves towards the oncoming wave, it moves closer to the next crest during the received period:

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0-uT_R.\]

Since the next crest still propagates by \(c_sT_R\),

\[c_sT_R=\lambda_0-uT_R.\]

Thus

\[f_R=f_0\left(1+\frac{u}{c_s}\right),\qquad \lambda_R=\lambda_0\frac{c_s}{c_s+u},\]

and again

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

8. Why this theme belongs in Path II

Sound gives a control case in which nobody needs a synchronization convention to know that the wave is propagating through a medium. The receiver moves through that propagating wave and changes its sequence of encounters.

It therefore makes the conceptual separation visible before Path II asks whether the same words — wave speed, message speed and receiver measurement — are being kept equally distinct for light.

Deep Notes

Why use sound at all? Because it supplies a physical control case with an explicit propagation medium. A sound wave is not a stationary spatial pattern waiting for a receiver to move through it: the wave itself propagates through the medium at \(c_s\). We can therefore follow the wave motion and receiver motion separately, then ask what spatial and temporal quantities belong to the receiver’s actual sequence of encounters.

1. Set up the wave in the medium frame

Let the source and medium be at rest. The source emits a periodic signal with

\[f_0,\qquad T_0=\frac{1}{f_0}.\]

Sound propagates through the medium at \(c_s\). The simultaneous separation of equal-phase fronts is therefore

\[\boxed{\lambda_0=c_sT_0=\frac{c_s}{f_0}}.\]

This is the ordinary medium-frame wavelength.

2. Define the receiver experiment

A receiver moves in the same direction as the wave at speed \(u\). Choose the instant when one crest reaches the receiver as the first reception event. At that same medium-frame instant, the next crest is one \(\lambda_0\) behind.

Let \(T_R\) be the time from this reception to the next equal-phase reception. During \(T_R\), the receiver moves

\[uT_R.\]

The next crest therefore has farther to travel than the initial crest separation.

3. Receding receiver: derive \(\lambda_R\) geometrically

The next crest begins \(\lambda_0\) behind and must additionally cover the distance by which the receiver recedes:

\[\boxed{\lambda_R\equiv\lambda_0+uT_R}.\]

This equation is the geometric definition of the receiver-side wavelength used on this page. It is the propagation distance between two equal-phase reception events.

The wave itself propagates at \(c_s\), so the same distance is

\[\boxed{\lambda_R=c_sT_R}.\]

These two statements describe the same trip of the next crest.

4. Derive \(T_R\) and \(f_R\)

Equating the two expressions,

\[c_sT_R=\lambda_0+uT_R,\]

gives

\[T_R=\frac{\lambda_0}{c_s-u}.\]

Hence

\[\boxed{f_R=\frac{1}{T_R}=\frac{c_s-u}{\lambda_0}=f_0\left(1-\frac{u}{c_s}\right)}.\]

5. Finish the independent derivation of \(\lambda_R\)

Use the geometric relation, not \(\lambda_R=c_s/f_R\), as the starting point:

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0+uT_R.\]

Substituting the derived \(T_R\),

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0+u\frac{\lambda_0}{c_s-u}=\lambda_0\frac{c_s}{c_s-u}.\]

Thus

\[\boxed{\lambda_R>\lambda_0\quad\text{for recession}.}\]

The larger \(\lambda_R\) does not mean the medium-frame snapshot wavelength has changed. It means the propagating crest must cover a larger distance between the receiver’s successive phase encounters because the receiver moves away while the crest is travelling.

6. Receiver-side product

Now the received frequency and receiver-side wavelength have been derived from the same moving-wave geometry:

\[f_R=f_0\left(1-\frac{u}{c_s}\right),\]
\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0\frac{c_s}{c_s-u}.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

This is the point of the construction: when the receiver’s own frequency is paired with the spatial propagation interval belonging to the same pair of reception events, the wave-propagation rate is recovered.

7. Keep \(\lambda_0\) and \(\lambda_R\) distinct

A medium-frame photograph still shows equal-phase fronts separated by \(\lambda_0\). Receiver motion does not physically stretch that snapshot pattern.

The two quantities answer different questions:

\(\lambda_0\): how far apart are equal-phase fronts at one medium-frame instant?

\(\lambda_R\): how far does the propagating wave travel from one equal-phase reception event to the next while the receiver is also moving?

Calling them both simply “the wavelength” without specifying the construction would hide the distinction. On this page, received wavelength means the second quantity.

8. The approach case

For a receiver moving towards the oncoming wave, the receiver shortens the distance the next crest must travel:

\[\lambda_R=\lambda_0-uT_R.\]

With \(\lambda_R=c_sT_R\),

\[T_R=\frac{\lambda_0}{c_s+u},\]

so

\[\boxed{f_R=f_0\left(1+\frac{u}{c_s}\right)},\]
\[\boxed{\lambda_R=\lambda_0\frac{c_s}{c_s+u}},\]

and again

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

9. Message arrival remains a separate quantity

A single marked pulse beginning a distance \(L\) behind a receding receiver satisfies

\[c_st=L+ut,\]

so

\[t_{msg}=\frac{L}{c_s-u},\qquad \frac{L}{t_{msg}}=c_s-u.\]

This is the message-arrival rate for that moving endpoint. It is not the sound-wave propagation speed through the medium.

10. What the sound control establishes

\[\boxed{\text{wave propagation }c_s\;\neq\;\text{message arrival }c_s\pm u\;\neq\;\text{medium-frame snapshot wavelength }\lambda_0.}\]

A moving receiver also has its own pair of received quantities, \(f_R\) and the receiver-side \(\lambda_R\), linked by

\[\boxed{f_R\lambda_R=c_s}.\]

Sound is included because this separation can be seen without invoking relativity. The next question is whether the same care is maintained when the messenger is light.
