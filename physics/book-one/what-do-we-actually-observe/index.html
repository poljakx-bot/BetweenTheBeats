Path I · Theme 1

What Do We Actually Observe?

Measurement begins with radiation received here. Source history is reconstructed through a causal and mathematical chain.

Published essay

Four questions to keep separate

Measurement is not the same thing as its explanation.

As you read, keep four tasks distinct: the standard account; what the measurement directly establishes; the portal’s objection or alternative; and the evidence that could distinguish the two.

Intuition

We never receive the event itself

When we say that we are looking into the past, several physical and interpretive steps have been compressed into one sentence. The distant event does not travel to us. Electromagnetic radiation propagates, reaches a detector, becomes a local material response, passes through processing, and is finally presented as an image, spectrum, catalogue entry, or fitted parameter.

source event ↓ emitted radiation ↓ propagation ↓ detector response ↓ processed data ↓ model-based reconstruction

A detector can measure received flux, observed frequencies, direction, polarisation, timing, and uncertainty. It does not directly read the source’s lookback time, luminosity distance, intrinsic luminosity, physical age, or complete emitted waveform. Those quantities are reconstructed from what arrived, together with a physical model.

What arrived is measured. What happened at the source is reconstructed.

This distinction does not make reconstruction arbitrary. It makes the causal chain visible.

The Argument

Remote astronomy is a measurement problem in which the source is not locally accessible. The instrument interacts only with radiation that has reached the detector.

1. What is directly observed?

A spectrograph provides a received quantity such as Fobs(λobs,tobs) together with calibration, noise, angular information, and detector response. These are properties of the received and processed signal.

2. What is inferred?

An identified feature is compared with a laboratory transition:

\[1 + z = \lambda_{obs} / \lambda_{emit} = \nu_{emit} / \nu_{obs}\]

From z, a cosmological model may infer luminosity distance, angular-diameter distance, lookback time, intrinsic luminosity, and physical size. The chain is therefore:

detector response → calibrated spectrum → feature identification → redshift → physical model → inferred source properties

3. Where interpretation enters

Interpretation enters through feature identification, a model of emission, a model of propagation, detector calibration, and the geometric relation used to convert redshift into distance and age.

4. The alternative question

Between the Beats asks whether part of the inferred cosmic history may instead belong to the history of the signal. It does not deny the measurements. It keeps the propagation operator visible in the inverse problem.

5. What is and is not claimed

The claim is that astronomical source history is an inverse reconstruction. It is not claimed that standard cosmology is false, that redshift is unreal, or that distant sources cannot be reliably identified.

Deep Notes

Remote astronomy begins with a local measurement

A distant astronomical event is not present at the detector when it is measured. What reaches the instrument is electromagnetic radiation. That radiation produces a local detector response, which is calibrated and processed into quantities such as flux, wavelength, polarisation, direction and arrival time.

The first distinction is therefore between properties of the received signal and properties reconstructed for the distant source. The second set can be extraordinarily well constrained. It is nevertheless inferred from the first.

This distinction is the reason remote astronomy can be treated formally as an inverse problem.

The forward problem

Suppose a distant source has a physical state S. Radiation produced by that source propagates toward the observer through some physical propagation process 𝓟. It then interacts with a detector described by an operator 𝓓.

\[O = \mathcal{D}[\mathcal{P}(S; \theta_{P}); \theta_{D}] + \varepsilon\]

Here S represents the source state, θP represents assumptions or parameters describing propagation, θD represents detector response and calibration, and ε represents noise and unmodelled uncertainty.

The forward question is: If the source and the path were known, what should arrive at the detector?

The inverse problem

Astronomy normally faces the opposite situation. The observation O is available. The original source state S is not.

The task is therefore to reconstruct the source from the received signal:

\[O \longrightarrow S\]

But this reconstruction requires assumptions about the mapping between them. It therefore has the conceptual form:

\[O \overset{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{D}}{\longrightarrow} S_{\rm inferred}\]

A successful inverse reconstruction is not a guess. It can be tightly constrained by calibration, independent measurements, repeated observations and predictions that agree across many different systems. The important point is simply that the reconstruction remains a reconstruction.

Redshift as an example

Suppose a spectral feature is observed at wavelength λobs. If that feature is identified with a laboratory transition having rest wavelength λemit, then the measured redshift is represented by:

\[1+z=\frac{\lambda_{obs}}{\lambda_{emit}}=\frac{\nu_{emit}}{\nu_{obs}}\]

The observed spectral position and the laboratory reference allow z to be determined. But quantities such as luminosity distance, angular-diameter distance and lookback time require an additional physical model relating z to the history and geometry of the universe.

detector response → calibrated spectrum → feature identification → redshift → physical model → source reconstruction

Each arrow can be tested. They should not be collapsed into one observational statement.

Identifiability

An inverse problem becomes especially important when more than one combination of source history and propagation history can produce approximately the same observation.

\[\mathcal{F}(S_1,P_1)\approx\mathcal{F}(S_2,P_2)\]

Then the received observation alone cannot uniquely distinguish (S1,P1) from (S2,P2). Additional information is required.

This is an identifiability problem. It does not mean that every reconstruction is equally plausible. Independent observables can strongly constrain the possibilities.

A property assigned to the source is secure only to the extent that alternative changes along the propagation path have also been constrained.

Why model dependence does not mean arbitrariness

A physical model may be model-dependent and still be extraordinarily successful. Standard cosmology combines many independent observations and imposes strong consistency requirements. A reconstruction that successfully predicts several unrelated observables is much more constrained than one fitted to a single spectrum.

\[\text{inferred}\neq\text{arbitrary}\]

But neither does a well-tested inference become a directly detected quantity. The epistemic distinction remains:

\[\text{directly measured quantity}\neq\text{quantity inferred through a tested model}\]

The propagation question

The question explored by Between the Beats is whether every systematic difference between the emitted and received signal should automatically be assigned to the source and cosmic geometry, or whether some portion could belong to the physical history of propagation itself.

Conceptually, instead of treating 𝓟 as already exhausted by the standard propagation model, the portal keeps it visible:

\[O=\mathcal{D}[\mathcal{P}(S)]\]

The question is therefore not whether the observation is real. It is: Could a different physically specified propagation operator reproduce part of what is presently reconstructed as source history?

That is a hypothesis to be tested, not an alternative conclusion assumed in advance.

What is not claimed

The distinction between observation and reconstruction does not establish that standard cosmology is wrong. It does not make redshift unreal. It does not imply that distances, ages or intrinsic luminosities are arbitrary. It does not establish that propagation contains an unrecognised effect.

It establishes only the logical structure of the measurement:

\[\text{what arrived}\neq\text{the reconstructed history of what emitted it}\]

Open obligation

A propagation-based alternative becomes scientifically useful only if it specifies its physical operator. It must predict how the signal changes as a function of propagation distance, frequency and source conditions. It must preserve observations that standard reconstruction already explains successfully.

And it must identify some observation for which the standard and alternative propagation models make different quantitative predictions.

Until then, keeping propagation visible in the inverse problem is a methodological point. A competing physical explanation begins only when the alternative operator can be calculated and tested.
