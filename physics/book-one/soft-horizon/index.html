The Soft Horizon: The Edge of Knowing

A source may remain detectable after its surviving signal no longer determines one unique source history.

Published essay

Four questions to keep separate

Measurement is not the same thing as its explanation.

As you read, keep four tasks distinct: the standard account; what the measurement directly establishes; the portal’s objection or alternative; and the evidence that could distinguish the two.

Intuition

The source disappears layer by layer

A source may contain a continuum, peaks, narrow lines, rapid fluctuations, and slow changes. As its frequency scale moves through the finite response of a receiver, those components do not all cross the boundary together.

\[f_{obs} = f_{emit}/(1+z)\]

For a receiver with lower limit fmin, a component reaches the boundary at:

\[1 + z_{h} = f_{emit}/f_{min}\]

Each emitted frequency therefore has its own observational horizon. The source can remain detectable after some of the information needed to identify it has been lost.

When reception becomes knowledge

The soft horizon begins as a boundary of reception. It becomes the edge of knowing when the surviving signal no longer determines one unique history of its source.

The event has not necessarily vanished. The unique path back to it has.

The Argument

1. A family of horizons

For features f₁,…,fₙ, each threshold zh,i differs. Higher-frequency components remain inside the same receiver band through a larger scaling factor than lower-frequency components.

2. The receiver is part of the boundary

The limit depends on bandwidth, sensitivity, spectral resolution, temporal resolution, observation duration, noise, absorption, and the material process that converts the arriving field into data. Another instrument may recover what the first has lost.

3. Scaling alone is reversible

If the full waveform is available without noise and the scale factor is known, the transformation can be reversed. The soft horizon arises from scaling plus finite reception, noise, attenuation, incomplete temporal coverage, or an unknown channel.

4. Detection without unique reconstruction

As identifying features disappear, several source-and-path combinations may become compatible with the same remaining observation. The source remains measured, but the inference becomes increasingly dependent on templates, population assumptions, and the cosmological model.

5. Three boundaries

An instrumental horizon belongs to one receiver. A reconstruction horizon is reached when the combined data no longer determine a stable source history. A fundamental horizon would require a physical reason why no possible receiver could recover the missing structure.

Deep Notes

A realistic observation operator

\[Y(f) = W(f)H(f,z)S((1+z)f) + N(f)\]

W represents finite receiver response, H propagation, and N noise. Reversing the redshift factor is not enough when W vanishes, H is uncertain, or noise dominates.

Inverse instability

A formal inverse divides by the channel response. Where |H| becomes small, a small observational uncertainty can produce a large change in the reconstructed source. The relevant boundary is not where Y becomes exactly zero, but where recovery ceases to have bounded useful uncertainty.

Information is not detected energy

A bright continuum may be easy to detect and poor at distinguishing source models. The edge of knowing is controlled by the discriminating structure that remains, not only by total received power.

Empirical program

A meaningful soft-horizon hypothesis must predict which identifying features disappear, when multi-band observation restores them, how classification uncertainty changes, and whether any residual limit remains after known instrumental and astrophysical selection effects are accounted for.
