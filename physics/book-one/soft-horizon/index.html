Path I · Theme 4

The Soft Horizon: The Edge of Knowing

A source can still be seen after the signal has lost enough distinguishing structure that its original history can no longer be reconstructed uniquely.

Published essay

The idea

Detection can survive identification.

A remaining signal can still carry energy and be statistically significant even after some of the features that told us what produced it have disappeared.

Intuition

A source does not disappear all at once

An astronomical signal can contain a continuum, narrow spectral lines, rapid variations, slow variations, polarisation and other identifying structure.

As the signal is shifted, attenuated, scattered or pushed toward the limits of a receiver, those pieces need not disappear together.

A broad component may remain visible after a narrow identifying feature is gone.

The source can remain detectable after the unique path back to its history has begun to disappear.

That is the soft horizon

The “horizon” here is not a hard wall where the universe suddenly becomes invisible. It is the point at which the surviving signal no longer carries enough independent structure to support one stable reconstruction of what produced it.

The Argument

1. A complex source carries many clues

Different spectral and temporal features carry different identifying information. A source can therefore lose one clue while retaining another.

2. The receiver has finite reach

If an emitted feature at frequency \(f_{emit}\) is observed at

\[f_{obs}=\frac{f_{emit}}{1+z},\]

then a receiver with a lower usable boundary \(f_{min}\) loses that feature when

\[\frac{f_{emit}}{1+z}

Another feature at a different emitted frequency crosses that boundary at a different redshift.

So the information content can erode progressively rather than vanish in one step.

3. Detection is easier than reconstruction

Detecting excess flux answers “is something there?” Reconstructing a source history asks the harder question “which source and which path produced this particular signal?”

The second question can fail while the first still succeeds.

4. The central distinction

\[\boxed{\text{detectable signal}\not\Rightarrow\text{uniquely reconstructable source history}.}\]

That is the whole soft-horizon argument.

Deep Notes

Start with a structured signal and a finite receiver. A distant source can be detected through many different features—continuum power, spectral lines, timing structure or polarisation. Those features need not survive reception equally well. The soft horizon is the point where enough identifying structure has been lost that the source may still be seen but no longer reconstructed uniquely.

1. A simple feature-dependent boundary

Take one emitted feature of frequency \(f_{emit}\). If the received frequency is related to it by

\[f_{obs}=\frac{f_{emit}}{1+z},\]

and the receiver is usable only above a lower frequency \(f_{min}\), that feature remains inside the band only while

\[\frac{f_{emit}}{1+z}\ge f_{min}.\]

The boundary for that feature is therefore

\[1+z_h=\frac{f_{emit}}{f_{min}}.\]

A second emitted feature at another frequency has a different \(z_h\). Even this simple example shows why a complex source need not disappear all at once.

2. Detection and identification use different information

A receiver may still register a statistically significant broad signal after one or more narrow identifying features have left its band or fallen below its sensitivity.

Detection asks whether received power or structure differs from background. Identification asks whether the surviving structure is specific enough to distinguish one source history from another. The second requirement is stronger.

3. Information is not the same as total power

A smooth continuum can remain bright while the line, timing feature or polarisation signature that separated two possible source models has disappeared.

The relevant loss is therefore not simply a loss of energy. It is a loss of discriminating structure.

4. An instrumental horizon is not automatically a physical one

If a different receiver operating in another band recovers the missing feature, the previous boundary belonged to the instrument. Better sensitivity, bandwidth or resolution can move such a boundary.

The stronger edge appears when the available observations no longer contain enough independent information to distinguish physically different source histories.

5. Why the horizon is soft

There need not be one distance or redshift where knowledge suddenly stops. One clue can weaken first, another later, until several different reconstructions become compatible with what remains.

\[\text{clear identification}\rightarrow\text{partial reconstruction}\rightarrow\text{multiple compatible histories}.\]

6. Final statement

\[\boxed{\text{detectability can survive after unique reconstruction has failed}.}\]

That separation—rather than a hard wall of zero signal—is what this page calls the soft horizon.
