From Titraj to D: What Remains After the Radiation?

Titraj treats electromagnetic radiation as an output of organised charge motion. Applied to an electron–positron encounter, that viewpoint raises a separate question: does the disappearance of the ordinary charged-particle signal after intense high-frequency emission establish that the two charges themselves have ceased to exist? The D hypothesis answers no — but that answer is an additional postulate, not a consequence already derived by Titraj.

Further hypothesis

Motivation → Additional postulate → Test

Radiation can be the output of a process without automatically identifying what remains after the process.

The standard QED account of electron–positron annihilation is highly successful and contains no stable D remnant. The purpose of this hypothesis is therefore not to rename the measured gamma output. It is to state a different final-state possibility and the experiment that would have to distinguish it.

Intuition

Start from the part Titraj actually motivates

Two opposite charges accelerating on a changing relative trajectory can produce electromagnetic radiation. In the Titraj programme, radiation is therefore treated first as an output of charge motion rather than as a mechanical substance into which the charges must be imagined to transform.

That observation alone does not change the standard electron–positron result. It only separates two questions that are usually answered together:

\[\text{What radiation was observed?}\]
\[\text{What physical state remained after that radiation?}\]

Standard physics already contains an electron–positron bound state

Positronium is a bound state of an electron and a positron. Standard QED predicts its spectroscopy and its annihilation decay channels with high precision. In the usual description, the electron–positron pair annihilates into allowed final states such as two or three gamma quanta, depending on the state. No long-lived neutral electron–positron remnant is included in that final state.

Positronium motivates taking the pre-annihilation pair seriously as a physical bound system. It does not by itself provide evidence for D after the decay.

The additional D postulate

D proposes that the radiative evolution can end before the two charges cease to exist:

\[e^-+e^+\longrightarrow \gamma\text{-output}+D,\]

with

\[D\equiv(e^-e^+)_{\rm post-radiation}.\]

Here \(\gamma\) is simply shorthand for the observed high-frequency electromagnetic output. The hypothesis does not require a classical projectile picture of photons.

D is proposed to be very nearly neutral, so ordinary charged-particle detection could become ineffective once the pair enters that configuration. This is a hypothesis about the final state, not an inference from detector silence.

The experiment has to make D visible again

If D contains an electron and a positron rather than nothing, a sufficiently strong external electromagnetic probe might disrupt the bound configuration. The clean experimental question becomes:

\[D+\gamma_{\rm probe}\longrightarrow e^-+e^+\;?\]

A positive result would require more than seeing a pair after a gamma pulse. The same probe can have ordinary backgrounds. The excess must depend on a previously created positron population and survive probe-only controls.

The Argument

The logical bridge from Titraj is limited but real

Path III proposes a general mechanism direction:

\[\text{organised charge motion}\longrightarrow\text{electromagnetic output}.\]

Applied to an approaching \(e^-e^+\) pair, that makes it natural to ask whether the measured gamma radiation is the radiative history of the pair rather than a complete mechanical description of what the pair became.

But the implication stops there:

\[\text{radiation from charge motion}\not\Rightarrow D.\]

D enters only with the additional statement

\[\boxed{\text{after the radiative stage, a bound }e^-e^+\text{ configuration remains}.}\]

The standard benchmark

In QED, positronium is an electron–positron bound state with precisely calculable annihilation channels and lifetimes. Para- and ortho-positronium have different allowed photon decay channels because of their quantum numbers. Modern positronium measurements are stringent tests of bound-state QED.

Any D model therefore has to reproduce the observed prompt and delayed annihilation spectra and rates before adding a hidden remnant. It cannot simply point to gamma emission as unexplained.

Why near-neutrality matters

A tightly correlated \(e^-e^+\) configuration would have small net charge at distances large compared with its internal separation. Qualitatively, this could make conventional charged-particle tracking much less sensitive than it is to a free electron or positron.

That observation is only a plausibility argument for difficult detection. It does not establish stability, size, binding energy or lifetime. Those are quantities a real D theory must calculate.

A discriminating probe experiment

Prepare a target in which a known positron population is stopped and allowed to pass through the ordinary prompt annihilation window. After a controlled delay, apply a probe pulse and compare at least two matched conditions:

\[\text{positron-loaded target + probe}\]
\[\text{same target + probe, but no prior positron loading}.\]

The probe-on/probe-off comparison is not enough by itself because the probe can generate ordinary backgrounds. A D signal must correlate with the earlier positron dose.

Possible observables include a delayed excess of correlated \(e^-e^+\) tracks where direct tracking is feasible, or a delayed secondary annihilation signature produced after a released positron re-enters ordinary matter.

A threshold would make the test stronger

If D has a definite binding scale \(E_D\), the breakup response should not rise arbitrarily with probe conditions. A future model should predict a dissociation threshold or response curve:

\[\Gamma_{D\rightarrow e^-e^+}=\mathcal F(f_{\rm probe},I_{\rm probe},E_D,G_D).\]

Scanning probe frequency and intensity would then test a predicted structure rather than merely search for an unspecified anomaly.

The exact status

\[\boxed{\text{Titraj motivates the question; D supplies a new final-state postulate; experiment must decide.}}\]

Deep Notes

D begins where the core Titraj argument ends. Titraj asks whether electromagnetic emission can be reconstructed as organised charge motion without making a travelling photon projectile the indispensable mechanical picture. Once that viewpoint is applied to an electron and a positron approaching one another, it becomes legitimate to separate the measured radiation from the ontology assigned to the final state.

That separation does not license an arbitrary remnant. Standard QED describes positronium and electron–positron annihilation with substantial quantitative success. D is therefore meaningful only if it produces a result that differs experimentally from the no-remnant final state while preserving the measurements already explained by QED.

The purpose of this note is to define that difference sharply.

Before the proposed remnant: positronium

Positronium provides an established example in which an electron and positron form a joint bound state before annihilation. Its singlet and triplet configurations have different dominant decay channels, and their rates and spectra are calculable within QED.

This matters conceptually because the constituents do not have to be treated as unrelated free charges until the final instant. A joint \(e^-e^+\) state is already part of established physics.

But positronium is not evidence that the same constituents remain after an ordinary annihilation decay. The standard final states contain the permitted decay products, not a hidden neutral \(e^-e^+\) residue.

The D modification

Write the standard final-state shorthand schematically as

\[e^-+e^+\longrightarrow \gamma\text{s}.\]

The D hypothesis changes it to

\[e^-+e^+\longrightarrow \gamma\text{s}+D.\]

The notation is intentionally modest. It does not yet specify whether the radiative stage is a spiral-like approach, another bounded trajectory, or a many-cycle reorganisation. Those details belong to a later dynamical model.

The new content is only that the observed radiative output is not the complete final state.

What D would have to conserve

A surviving remnant immediately creates strict conservation requirements. Its rest energy, binding energy, recoil momentum and any internal excitation must be included together with the measured radiation:

\[P^\mu_{initial}=P^\mu_{radiation}+P^\mu_D.\]

A viable model cannot leave the usual annihilation photon energies unchanged and then add an arbitrary massive remnant without accounting for the corresponding energy–momentum balance. This is one of the strongest quantitative obligations of D.

Therefore the portal does not claim that an experimentally standard two-gamma event can simply contain an energetically invisible extra object. The spectrum and recoil accounting have to be recalculated once a definite D mass and binding energy are proposed.

Why detector silence is insufficient

Near-neutrality could suppress direct electromagnetic tracking at distances above the internal separation, but absence of a charged track is not evidence for D. Neutral matter is detected in many indirect ways, and a new bound state could interact with matter through polarisation, scattering, breakup or annihilation channels.

D therefore needs a positive signature rather than an argument from invisibility.

The delayed breakup test

The useful experimental topology is temporal:

\[\text{positron loading}\rightarrow\text{ordinary annihilation window}\rightarrow\Delta t\rightarrow\text{probe}\rightarrow\text{search}.\]

If a D population survives, its abundance after the delay can be written schematically as

\[N_D(\Delta t)=N_D(0)e^{-\Delta t/\tau_D}\]

for a simple single-lifetime model. A probe-induced signal should then scale with both the surviving D population and the breakup response:

\[R_{probe}(\Delta t)\propto N_D(\Delta t)\,\Gamma_{break}(f_{probe},I_{probe}).\]

This gives several independent handles: positron dose, delay time, probe frequency, probe intensity and target material.

Controls are part of the hypothesis

A high-energy electromagnetic probe can itself create backgrounds, including ordinary electron–positron production when kinematically and materially allowed. The experiment therefore requires matched controls:

  • probe with no prior positron loading;
  • positron loading with no delayed probe;
  • delay scans to test whether any excess follows a reproducible lifetime;
  • probe-frequency or intensity scans to search for a breakup threshold;
  • target changes to test whether any stored population depends on material environment.

The discriminating signal is not “a pair appeared after gamma illumination.” It is an excess tied jointly to prior positron loading, delay and the predicted breakup response.

What this hypothesis does not claim

D is not being used here to reinterpret Breit–Wheeler or collider pair-production measurements. Pair creation is a different experimental question and does not belong in the core motivation for D.

Nor does this first note claim that D explains anomalous beta decays, nuclear transmutation or excess radioactivity. Those would be downstream hypotheses requiring separate evidence if D itself were ever established.

The clean falsifiable statement

A completed D model must predict at least a remnant mass or binding scale, a lifetime, an interaction or breakup cross-section, and the resulting modification — however small — to the annihilation energy–momentum accounting.

Only then can the delayed-probe experiment be made quantitative:

\[\boxed{\text{prior }e^+\text{ population}+\text{delay}+\text{probe}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{predicted excess response if D exists}.}\]

The boundary

The established observation is electron–positron annihilation radiation and the accurately measured behaviour of positronium. The Titraj connection supplies a reason to reopen the mechanical question of what radiation represents. D is the separate proposal that something remains. Until it produces a quantitative final-state model and a positive differential signal, it should be treated as a further hypothesis rather than part of the core reconstruction.
